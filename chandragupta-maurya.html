Chandragupta Maurya

Founder of the Maurya Empire · Conqueror of the Nanda Empire · Jain Renunciate

In one line: The founder of the first pan-subcontinental empire in Indian history, who overthrew the Nandas, defeated the Seleucid Greeks, and ended his life as a Jain ascetic.

Quick Facts

Reign c. 322–297 BCE (Puranas list his reign as 24 years)
Dynasty Maurya
Imperial Capital Pataliputra (modern Patna, Bihar)
Predecessor Dhana Nanda (overthrown)
Successor Bindusara
Contemporary Figures Chanakya (Kautilya), Seleucus I Nicator, Megasthenes

Life & Reign

The origin and early youth of Chandragupta Maurya remain among the most vigorously debated subjects in ancient South Asian historiography. Brahmanical Puranic traditions, compiled centuries later, suggest a humble or base origin, with later commentators attempting an etymological derivation of ‘Maurya’ from Mura, alleged to be a Shudra concubine of a Nanda monarch. Conversely, Buddhist chronicles such as the Mahavamsa and Divyavadana describe him as a scion of the noble Moriya clan of Pipphalivana — a branch of the solar Kshatriya lineage associated with the Sakyas. Jain traditions recorded by Hemachandra in the Parisishtaparvan connect his ancestry to a community of royal peacock-tamers (mayura-poshaka). Roman historian Justin, drawing on earlier Hellenistic accounts, describes him simply as a man born of humble station (novus homo) who was propelled to imperial destiny by divine signs and extraordinary personal daring.

A foundational turning point occurred in the northwestern borderlands of Taxila, where the young Chandragupta encountered Chanakya (also known as Kautilya or Vishnugupta), a brilliant Brahmin scholar of statecraft who had been publicly insulted by King Dhana Nanda in the royal court of Pataliputra. Bound by a mutual ambition to topple the corrupt and heavily taxing Nanda dynasty, Chanakya recognized Chandragupta’s natural military charisma and took him to Taxila. There, Chandragupta was educated in military tactics, diplomacy, and administrative governance. As Alexander of Macedon retreated from the Punjab following the mutiny of his troops at the Hyphasis (Beas) in 326 BCE, Chandragupta and Chanakya assembled an army comprising regional mercenaries, republican warriors, forest tribes (atavikas), and northwestern warlords to exploit the power vacuum left by Macedonian withdrawals.

Around 321 BCE, Chandragupta launched his decisive offensive against the heartland of Magadha. As dramatized in Vishakhadatta’s classical political play Mudrarakshasa (“The Signet Ring of Rakshasa”), the overthrow of Dhana Nanda was not merely an open military confrontation, but an intricate campaign of political subversion, intelligence warfare, and calculated assassinations orchestrated by Chanakya. Initial frontal assaults on the heavily defended Nanda capital of Pataliputra had failed because Chandragupta had attacked the core without securing the periphery — an error Chanakya famously corrected by observing a mother scolding her child for eating the scorching center of a hot pastry rather than nibbling the cooler outer crust. Operating systematically from the borders inward, Chandragupta encircled Pataliputra, assassinated Dhana Nanda, co-opted the loyal and capable Nanda prime minister Rakshasa to preserve administrative continuity, and proclaimed the Maurya Empire.

Having consolidated northern India, Chandragupta turned his military attention westward to confront Seleucus I Nicator, Alexander’s leading general who had reunited the eastern satrapies of the Macedonian Empire and crossed the Indus around 305 BCE to reclaim Alexander’s Indian conquests. Greco-Roman historians, including Appian, Plutarch, and Justin, record that Chandragupta confronted Seleucus with an overwhelming military force. Plutarch notes that Chandragupta overran all of India with an army of 600,000 men. The ensuing conflict concluded with a decisive peace treaty highly favorable to the Mauryans. Seleucus ceded the vast strategic satrapies of Arachosia (Kandahar), Gedrosia (Balochistan), Paropamisadae (Kabul), and Aria (Herat), effectively pushing the Mauryan imperial frontier to the Hindu Kush mountains. In exchange, Chandragupta presented Seleucus with 500 war elephants — beasts that proved instrumental in Seleucus’s victory at the Battle of Ipsus in Phrygia (301 BCE) — and cemented the alliance through an “epigamia” (marital alliance), accompanied by the dispatch of the Greek ambassador Megasthenes to the Mauryan court at Pataliputra.

Under Chandragupta and Chanakya, the Mauryan realm was organized into the most sophisticated, highly centralized administrative apparatus yet seen in Asia, codifying the principles later compiled in the Arthashastra. Megasthenes, recording his eyewitness observations in his Indica, described Pataliputra as a magnificent fortified metropolis enclosed by a wooden palisade with 570 towers and 64 gates, governed by a municipal commission of thirty members divided into six specialized boards (overseeing industrial arts, foreign visitors, vital statistics, commercial sales, weights and measures, and excise tax collection). The agrarian economy was meticulously surveyed, state monopolies were enforced over mining, salt, and forestry, and an omnipresent espionage bureau (gudhapurushas) monitored provincial officials, military commanders, and public sentiment to detect treason before it could organize.

The Mauryan state also directed extensive public civil engineering and hydraulic irrigation projects. As confirmed by the second-century CE Junagadh rock inscription of Western Kshatrapa ruler Rudradaman, Chandragupta’s provincial governor (Rashtriya) in Saurashtra (Gujarat), Pushyagupta Vaishya, dammed local rivers to construct the monumental Sudarshana Lake reservoir — an engineered water infrastructure project that supplied canal irrigation to the arid Kathiawar peninsula for centuries.

In the twilight of his reign, northern India was struck by a catastrophic twelve-year famine, documented prominently in Digambara Jain traditions. According to Hemachandra’s twelfth-century chronicle Parisishtaparvan and medieval Kannada epigraphic records at Shravanabelagola, the Jain spiritual master Acharya Bhadrabahu foresaw the prolonged agricultural collapse. Convinced that the imperial state could neither avert the famine nor uphold moral purity amid mass starvation, Chandragupta chose to abdicate the throne in favor of his son Bindusara around 297 BCE.

Renouncing imperial pomp, wealth, and sovereign authority, the emperor took the vows of a Digambara Jain monk and migrated south with Bhadrabahu and twelve thousand Jain ascetics to the hill of Chandragiri at Shravanabelagola (in modern Hassan district, Karnataka). There, living as a solitary recluse in a mountain cave, Chandragupta concluded his life through the ancient Jain ritual practice of sallekhana (holy voluntary fasting unto death). While modern historians debate the exact degree to which later Jain monastic hagiography amplified his renunciation, the unbroken presence of early Kannada epigraphs at Chandragiri Hill associating the site with “Bhadrabahu and Chandragupta Muni” provides tangible testimony to an imperial sovereign who laid down an empire to embrace monastic austerity.

Documented Achievements

  • Founding of the Maurya Empire: Overthrew the Nanda dynasty and established the first pan-subcontinental empire in Indian history, unifying fractured kingdoms into a single sovereign realm.
  • Defeat of Seleucus I Nicator: Repelled the Seleucid Greek invasion c. 305 BCE, securing the Indus Valley, Gandhara, and eastern Afghanistan (Arachosia, Gedrosia, Paropamisadae, Aria).
  • Centralized Administrative State: Created a highly organized administrative and bureaucratic structure codified in the Arthashastra, governed by specialized boards and rigorous auditing.
  • Pan-Indian Political Unification: Unified northern, northwestern, and central India into one political unit for the first time, extending imperial boundaries from Bengal to the Hindu Kush.
  • Public Hydraulic Engineering: Supervised the construction of the massive Sudarshana Lake irrigation reservoir in Saurashtra via his governor Pushyagupta Vaishya (Junagadh inscription).
  • International Diplomatic Relations: Established enduring diplomatic exchanges with the Hellenistic Mediterranean, welcoming Greek ambassador Megasthenes to the royal court at Pataliputra.

Documented Atrocities & Controversies

  • Brutal Conquest and Dynastic Liquidation: Overthrew the Nanda dynasty through violent warfare, palace intrigues, and assassinations of royal claimants and recalcitrant provincial rulers.
  • Extensive Espionage and State Terror: Maintained an all-pervasive surveillance state (gudhapurusha network) described in the Arthashastra, utilizing poisoners, secret agents, and political assassinations to eliminate internal dissidents.
  • Militarized Standing Force: Maintained an enormous standing army recorded by Megasthenes and Pliny as numbering 600,000 infantry, 30,000 cavalry, and 9,000 war elephants, financed through heavy agrarian levies.
  • The 12-Year Famine and Political Purges: Jain sources record severe social devastation and economic collapse during a catastrophic twelve-year famine, which precipitated palace crises and political purges.
  • Debate on Abdication and Sallekhana: Modern historians debate whether his abdication and starvation death (sallekhana) at Shravanabelagola reflect historic reality or later Jain monastic hagiography attempting to claim the imperial founder.

Primary Evidence & Sources

Evidence — EB-CM-1: Origins, Chanakya & Overthrow of the Nandas
  • Literary (Drama): Vishakhadatta’s Mudrārākṣasa (“The Signet Ring of Rakshasa”) details the political strategies, intelligence networks, and assassinations orchestrated by Chanakya to depose the Nandas and secure Chandragupta’s throne.
  • Buddhist Chronicles: The Mahāvaṃsa (Pali chronicle) and Mahāvaṃsa-ṭīkā record Chandragupta’s affiliation with the Kshatriya Moriya clan of Pipphalivana and his mentorship under Chanakya at Taxila.
  • Puranic Lists: The Vāyu, Matsya, and Viṣṇu Purāṇas record that Kautilya uprooted the nine Nandas and anointed Chandragupta king, assigning him a reign of 24 years.
Limitations: Mudrarakshasa: Written much later, usually dated to the 5th century CE, so it is a later literary source, not a contemporary record. Puranas: Religious/legendary compilations; chronology must be checked against Greek evidence.
Evidence — EB-CM-2: The Seleucid War & Territorial Treaty (c. 305 BCE)
  • Hellenistic Historiography: Appian (Syriaca, 55) records Seleucus crossing the Indus, waging war against “Androcottus” (Chandragupta), and concluding an agreement involving an “epigamia” (marriage alliance) and the transfer of 500 war elephants.
  • Classical Biographies: Plutarch (Life of Alexander, 62) records that Androcottus overran all India with 600,000 men and later gifted 500 war elephants to Seleucus; Justin (Epitome of the Philippic History of Pompeius Trogus, 15.4) describes Chandragupta’s rise and his conflict with Seleucus.
  • Diplomatic Envoy: Strabo (Geographica, 15.1.36) and Arrian cite Megasthenes, who was dispatched by Seleucus as official ambassador to Chandragupta’s court at Pataliputra.
Limitations: Indica: The original is lost; we have only later quotations and summaries.
Evidence — EB-CM-3: Administrative Apparatus, Espionage & Urban Governance
  • Political Treatise: Kautilya’s Arthaśāstra outlines a complete theoretical apparatus of imperial statecraft: the seven components of state (saptāṅga), state monopolies, judicial torture, the secret service (saṃsthās and sañcāras), and fiscal taxation.
  • Eyewitness Municipal Records: Fragments of Megasthenes’ Indica describing the six municipal governing committees of Pataliputra, the military administration overseen by six boards of five members, and the royal palace guard consisting of armed female bodyguards.
  • Archaeological Excavations: Excavations at Kumrahar and Bulandi Bagh in Patna (Waddell, Spooner) uncovered monumental Mauryan wooden palisades, defensive ramparts, and a multi-pillared hall matching Megasthenes’ description of Pataliputra.
Limitations: Arthashastra: Its exact date and direct connection to Chandragupta are debated; it is more a manual of governance than a biography. Indica: The original is lost; we have only later quotations and summaries.
Evidence — EB-CM-4: Jain Conversion, Famine & Sallekhana at Shravanabelagola
  • Jain Hagiography: Hemachandra’s Pariśiṣṭaparvan (12th c. CE) details the 12-year famine in Magadha, Chandragupta’s abdication in favor of Bindusara, and his migration south with Bhadrabahu.
  • Epigraphic Attestations: Inscriptions on Chandragiri Hill at Shravanabelagola (dating from the 7th century CE onward, notably the c. 600 CE inscription) commemorate the holy pair Bhadrabahu-swami and the disciple king Chandragupta-muni who died by sallekhana.
  • Archaeological Heritage: The Chandragupta Basti and Bhadrabahu Cave preserved atop Chandragiri Hill represent an unbroken pilgrimage tradition connecting the site with the Mauryan emperor.
Limitations: Jain texts: Composed centuries after his life; useful for tradition, not precise political facts.
Evidence — EB-CM-5: The Junagadh Rock Inscription of Rudradaman
  • Epigraphic Monument: The Junagadh rock inscription of Western Kshatrapa Mahakshatrapa Rudradaman I (dated to 150 CE), carved on a massive granitic rock at the foot of Mount Girnar in Gujarat.
  • Explicit Historical Attestation: Line 8 of the Sanskrit inscription explicitly names Chandragupta Maurya (Candraguptasya mauryasya) and records that his provincial governor (Rāṣṭrīya), Pushyagupta the Vaishya, built the dam for the historic Sudarshana Lake reservoir.
  • Corroborating Governance: The inscription also notes that Ashoka’s Yavana governor Tushaspha subsequently added canal conduits, confirming unbroken Mauryan administrative stewardship over western India.
Limitations: Junagadh inscription: It is from the 2nd century CE, not Chandragupta’s own reign.

Key Contemporaries

Chanakya (Kautilya)

Chief Minister · Statesman

Master strategist, author of the Arthashastra, and mentor who orchestrated the military and diplomatic overthrow of the Nanda dynasty.

Seleucus I Nicator

Hellenistic Emperor · Rival & Ally

Macedonian general and founder of the Seleucid Empire who fought Chandragupta c. 305 BCE, ceding eastern satrapies in exchange for 500 war elephants.

Megasthenes

Greek Ambassador · Author of Indica

Seleucid diplomat who resided at Pataliputra, composing the Indica, the earliest comprehensive foreign eyewitness account of South Asian society and administration.

Dhana Nanda

Last Nanda Monarch · Antagonist

Wealthy emperor of Magadha whose tyrannical governance and massive army were ultimately crushed by Chandragupta and Chanakya c. 321 BCE.

Acharya Bhadrabahu

Jain Spiritual Leader · Monk

Last Shrutakevali of the Jain tradition who led the monastic migration to Shravanabelagola during the 12-year famine, initiating Chandragupta into asceticism.

Pushyagupta Vaishya

Governor (Rashtriya) of Saurashtra

Imperial provincial administrator who engineered the historic Sudarshana Lake dam in Gujarat, documented in the Junagadh rock inscription.

Historical Legacy

Chandragupta Maurya’s founding of the Maurya Empire fundamentally reshaped the geopolitical identity of South Asia. Prior to his conquests, the subcontinent was fractured into dozens of competing mahājanapadas, tribal republics, and regional monarchies. By unifying the Gangetic plain with the Indus basin and the northwestern borderlands, Chandragupta established the political archetype of the chakravartin — the universal sovereign ruler whose hegemony encompassed the entirety of ‘Bharatavarsha’.

His victory over Seleucus I Nicator established the Hindu Kush as the historic geopolitical boundary of the Indian world, checking Hellenistic eastward expansion and inaugurating two centuries of intense cultural, commercial, and diplomatic exchange between India and the Mediterranean basin. The municipal and economic infrastructure described by Megasthenes and the Arthashastra provided the institutional scaffolding upon which his grandson Ashoka would later erect his ethical empire of Dhamma.

In subcontinental historical consciousness, Chandragupta occupies a dual status: the iron-willed conqueror who forged the subcontinent’s first military superpower through realpolitik and intelligence networks, and the supreme ascetic who relinquished supreme sovereign authority to die of voluntary fasting upon a bare granite rock in Karnataka. His life thus spans the entire ideological spectrum of classical Indian thought — from Kautilyan artha (material power) to Jain moksha (spiritual liberation).
