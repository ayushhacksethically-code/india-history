1757–1947 · AND BEYOND

The Freedom Struggle

Many streams, one direction — a 190-year story

In one line: India’s resistance to colonial rule lasted nearly two centuries and included many parallel struggles — political, caste, tribal, gender, labour, and religious — not just one Congress-led movement.

The Plural Meaning of Swaraj

Freedom from What?

In one line: Real independence required dismantling external British imperial subjugation and internal feudal, caste, and gender tyrannies simultaneously.

For decades, popular narratives reduced India’s independence struggle to a single, monolithic track: the Indian National Congress petitioned, Mahatma Gandhi led non-violent mass marches, and in August 1947 the British departed. While the transfer of sovereign political authority was a momentous victory, it represents only the most visible surface layer of a much deeper, multifaceted civilizational revolution.

To the vast majority of people across the Indian subcontinent — agricultural tenants, landless agricultural labourers, Dalits, adivasis, industrial workers, and women — British colonial rule was only the outer ring of oppression. A true liberation (Swaraj) demanded not merely replacing white British rulers with brown Indian elites, but dismantling domestic hierarchies that had entrenched inequality for centuries. The Indian freedom movement was therefore never one stream; it was a mighty confluence of seven interconnected, sometimes cooperating and sometimes clashing, emancipatory struggles.

Layer 1

Political Freedom

Liberation from direct British Crown sovereignty, imperial military occupation, racial discrimination, and colonial disenfranchisement.

Layer 2

Economic Freedom

Emancipation of the impoverished peasantry from predatory colonial land revenue, moneylender debt bondage, and feudal zamindari exploitation.

Layer 3

Caste Freedom

Dismantling untouchability, upper-caste social monopolies, and the hereditary ritual tyranny of graded caste inequality.

Layer 4

Tribal Freedom

Defense of ancestral indigenous autonomy and customary rights over Jal, Jangal, Zameen against colonial forest laws and commercial encroachment.

Layer 5

Gender Freedom

Overcoming deep-seated patriarchal domination, purdah, child marriage, and denial of education and property rights through active female leadership.

Layer 6

Religious Freedom

Preserving syncretic subcontinental pluralism, protecting minority rights, and resisting British divide-and-rule communal polarization.

Layer 7

Labour Freedom

Organizing industrial mill-hands, tea plantation coolies, and railway workers against brutal sweatshop conditions, wage theft, and union suppression.

Nationalist Trajectory

The Visible Layer — Political Resistance (1757–1947)

In one line: Over 190 years, anti-colonial political resistance evolved from localized rebellions and elite constitutional petitioning into mass civil disobedience and revolutionary defiance.

The political struggle against British rule unfolded across distinct phases. In the century following the Battle of Plassey (1757), resistance was fierce but regionalized — from the Sanyasi and Fakir uprisings in Bengal to the Paika Rebellion of 1817 in Odisha and Kittur Chennamma’s defiance in Karnataka. This scattered resistance culminated in the monumental convulsion of the Great Rebellion of 1857, which unified sepoys, dispossessed princes, and peasants across the Gangetic plains before being violently suppressed.

The birth of organized all-India constitutional nationalism arrived in December 1885 with the founding of the Indian National Congress by A.O. Hume, W.C. Bonnerjee, and Dadabhai Naoroji. Initially pursuing moderate tactics of “prayer, petition, and protest,” the nationalist movement was radically altered in 1905 when Viceroy Lord Curzon announced the Partition of Bengal along communal lines. The resulting Swadeshi movement — championed by the militant triumvirate of Bal Gangadhar Tilak (“Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it”), Bipin Chandra Pal, and Lala Lajpat Rai — introduced economic boycotts, national education, and revolutionary secret societies.

The turning point toward mass mobilisation came with the return of Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi from South Africa in 1915. Following the catastrophic massacre of hundreds of peaceful demonstrators at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar by Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer in April 1919, Gandhi launched the Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922) in alliance with the Khilafat cause. The 1930 Dandi Salt March transformed salt — a universal staple taxed by the Crown — into a global symbol of civil disobedience. Alongside non-violence, revolutionary socialists like Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Chandrashekhar Azad electrified the youth by demonstrating that genuine liberation required an end to the exploitation of man by man.

The final decade witnessed unprecedented urgency. In August 1942, the Congress passed the historic Quit India Resolution in Bombay, with Gandhi delivering the rallying cry “Do or Die,” precipitating spontaneous mass uprisings across the country. Simultaneously, Subhas Chandra Bose organized the Indian National Army (Azad Hind Fauj) from Southeast Asia, demonstrating that Indian soldiers were ready to take up arms against imperial rule. When three captured INA officers — Prem Kumar Sahgal, Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon, and Shah Nawaz Khan — were put on trial at the Red Fort in 1945, the entire nation rallied in protest. In February 1946, ratings of the Royal Indian Navy mutinied in Bombay and Karachi, signaling to the British war cabinet that their ultimate instrument of coercive rule — the armed forces — could no longer be trusted. Independence was won on 15 August 1947.

“Here is a mantra, a short one, that I give you. You may imprint it on your hearts and let every breath of yours give expression to it. The mantra is: ‘Do or Die.’ We shall either free India or die in the attempt; we shall not live to see the perpetuation of our slavery.”
Mahatma Gandhi, Speech to the All India Congress Committee, Bombay (8 August 1942)
Living Connection

The INC still exists. The Dandi March is still re-enacted. Quit India’s slogan “Do or Die” is still quoted. 1947 is not just history — it is living memory for millions of families.

Six Subaltern Streams

The Parallel Layers of Emancipation

In one line: Alongside the battle against the British Raj, millions of Indians fought simultaneously for caste annihilation, indigenous land rights, gender equality, workers’ dignity, peasant relief, and religious solidarity.

A) Caste Freedom: Phule, Ambedkar, and Periyar

The anti-caste movement insisted that political independence without social democracy would merely substitute British autocracy with Brahminical supremacy. In Maharashtra, Jyotirao Phule challenged the divine justification of caste hierarchy through his radical text Gulamgiri (Slavery, 1873), dedicating it to the abolitionists of the American Civil War. In the twentieth century, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar organized the historic Mahad Satyagraha (1927) to assert the fundamental right of Untouchables to drink from public municipal water reservoirs, publicly burning the ancient legal text Manusmriti. At the Round Table Conferences in London, Ambedkar clashed with Gandhi, demanding separate electorates for the Depressed Classes to guarantee autonomous political voice — a dispute resolved through the tense compromise of the Poona Pact (1932). In the Tamil south, Periyar E.V. Ramasamy launched the Self-Respect Movement (1925), waging uncompromising warfare against priestly domination and gender subjugation.

“Political democracy cannot last unless there lies at the base of it social democracy. What does social democracy mean? It means a way of life which recognizes liberty, equality, and fraternity as the principles of life.”
Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, Speech in the Constituent Assembly (25 November 1949)

B) Tribal Freedom: Birsa Munda, Alluri, and the Santhal Hul

India’s adivasi communities fought against colonialism long before urban nationalist associations existed. In June 1855, over 30,000 Santhal villagers led by the brothers Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu mobilized in the Santhal Hul (Rebellion) across Bihar and Bengal, armed with bows and arrows against British troops and exploitative moneylenders (dikus). In the Chhota Nagpur plateau, Birsa Munda led the Ulgulan (The Great Tumult, 1899–1900), mobilizing Munda peasants against British land revenue laws and Christian missionary interference to proclaim Munda Raj. In the hills of Andhra Pradesh, Alluri Sitarama Raju mobilized Rampa tribal fighters (1922–1924) in guerrilla warfare against the oppressive Madras Forest Act of 1882, which outlawed their customary shifting cultivation. In the northeastern hills, young Rani Gaidinliu led the Heraka movement against British rule in Manipur and Nagaland before being imprisoned for fourteen years.

C) Gender Freedom: Pandita Ramabai, Sarojini Naidu, and Aruna Asaf Ali

Women were neither passive beneficiaries nor mere auxiliaries of the freedom struggle; they were its strategists and frontline combatants. Pioneer intellectual Pandita Ramabai testified before the Hunter Education Commission in 1882, advocating women doctors and teachers while defying orthodox Hindu and colonial patriarchal codes. Sarojini Naidu became the first Indian woman president of the Indian National Congress in 1925, famously leading the hazardous salt raid on the Dharasana Salt Works in May 1930 after Gandhi’s arrest. Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay persuaded Gandhi to recruit women into the Salt Satyagraha and emerged as a global socialist champion of women’s suffrage. During the 1942 Quit India crackdown, while male leaders were jailed, thirty-three-year-old Aruna Asaf Ali hoisted the National Tricolour at the Gowalia Tank maidan in Bombay, evading police underground for years. In Bengal, young revolutionaries like Pritilata Waddedar and Bina Das took up arms, while in the INA, Captain Lakshmi Sahgal commanded the all-female Rani of Jhansi Regiment.

D) Labour Freedom: The Bombay Textile Strikes and the AITUC

The industrial transformation of Bombay, Calcutta, Ahmedabad, and Madras created an exploited urban proletariat working 14- to 16-hour shifts for starvation wages. Organized labour resistance surged following the First World War. In 1918, B.P. Wadia founded the Madras Labour Union, the subcontinent’s first modern trade union. In October 1920, nationalist leaders including Lala Lajpat Rai and N.M. Joshi established the All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC). The historic 1928 Bombay Textile General Strike, led by the militant Girni Kamgar Union under communist pioneers S.A. Dange and Shaukat Usmani, shut down the city’s cotton mills for six continuous months. In Madras, M. Singaravelu organized the first May Day celebration in 1923, linking anti-imperialism directly with the international working-class struggle.

E) Economic Freedom: Champaran, Kheda, and Bardoli

Mahatma Gandhi’s earliest political triumphs on Indian soil were grassroots agrarian class struggles. In Champaran (1917), Bihar, he mobilized tenant farmers against European plantation owners who legally coerced them to cultivate indigo on 3/20ths of their land (the tinkathia system). In Kheda (1918), Gujarat, alongside Vallabhbhai Patel, he organized peasants to withhold land taxes when catastrophic monsoon failure caused total crop loss. A decade later, the Bardoli Satyagraha (1928) organized 85,000 farmers in total non-violent refusal to pay an unjustified 22 percent land revenue increase, compelling the colonial government to roll back the hike and earning Vallabhbhai Patel the title of Sardar. In the 1930s, Swami Sahajanand Saraswati founded the All India Kisan Sabha in Bihar, mobilizing millions of peasant cultivators against the feudal zamindari apparatus.

F) Religious Freedom: Maulana Azad, Ghaffar Khan, and the Akalis

The freedom struggle depended profoundly upon patriotic religious leaders who rejected British attempts to divide the nation along sectarian lines. Islamic scholar Maulana Abul Kalam Azad served as Congress President during the critical wartime years (1940–1946), arguing that Indian Muslims were organic, inseparable citizens of a composite subcontinental nationhood (*Ummatt-un-Wahida*). Across the North-West Frontier, Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan (“The Frontier Gandhi”) mobilized over 100,000 ethnic Pashtuns into the Khudai Khidmatgar (“Servants of God”), practicing strict non-violence in the face of brutal colonial police firings. In the Punjab, the Akali Movement (1920–1925) waged non-violent mass agitation to wrest control of historic Sikh Gurdwaras from corrupt British-backed mahants, demonstrating how faith-based mobilization could fortify democratic anti-colonial solidarity.

Tragic Division

Partition — The Cost of Freedom

In one line: Independence arrived hand-in-hand with the catastrophic, blood-stained division of the subcontinent, creating the largest refugee crisis in human history.

Midnight of 14–15 August 1947 was a moment of triumphant liberation, celebrated by Jawaharlal Nehru’s timeless “Tryst with Destiny” address in New Delhi. Yet for tens of millions of people in Punjab, Bengal, Sindh, and the North-West Frontier, freedom arrived not as rejoicing, but as apocalyptic trauma. The partition of British India into the independent dominions of India and Pakistan unleashed unprecedented human destruction.

Between 14 and 15 million people were violently uprooted from ancestral homelands in the largest mass migration across international borders in recorded human history. Estimates of civilian deaths range from approximately 200,000 (contemporary British colonial figures; Penderel Moon) to over 1,000,000 (standard historical scholarship; Talbot & Singh 2009; Brass 2003), with some estimates as high as 2 million. The exact toll remains one of the most contested numbers in South Asian history. Over 75,000 women were abducted, raped, and subjected to sexual violence on both sides of the contested borders.

Evidence
  • Contemporary British colonial figures: Penderel Moon.
  • Modern scholarship: Talbot and Singh (2009), The Partition of India; Brass (2003); Bharadwaj, Khwaja, and Mian (2008).
  • Demographic reconstruction: Hill et al. (2008), “The Demographic Impact of Partition.”
Limitations: Death tolls remain among the most contested numbers in modern South Asian history; no census-based figure is available.

The geographic borders were hastily drawn by British barrister Sir Cyril Radcliffe, who had never visited the Indian subcontinent prior to his appointment. Working with outdated census maps and given a mere 36 days to bisect ancient ecological and cultural regions, Radcliffe drew the border lines in secret; the British administration delayed publication of the boundary awards until two days after independence to escape administrative responsibility for the ensuing carnage.

In Delhi, fireworks lit the night sky; but in Calcutta, Mahatma Gandhi spent Independence Day in fasting and prayer in an abandoned house in Beliaghata, striving through moral presence to quell sectarian riots. The trauma of Partition remains an open wound across South Asia — a devastating reminder that freedom arrived coupled with irreparable human rupture.

“Long years ago we made a tryst with destiny, and now the time comes when we shall redeem our pledge, not wholly or in full measure, but very substantially. At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom.”
Jawaharlal Nehru, “Tryst with Destiny” Address to the Indian Constituent Assembly (14 August 1947)
“Not all communities won freedom in the same way. For many, 1947 was displacement, loss, and exile, not liberation.”
Historiographical Consensus on Partition Oral Histories
Ongoing Battles

Unfinished Struggles (1947–Present)

In one line: The achievement of sovereign national independence in 1947 was not the end of struggle, but the starting point for ongoing democratic battles for genuine equality and civil dignity.

The departure of the British Raj transferred the instruments of state power to the Indian people, but it did not automatically eliminate structural injustice. Over the decades since 1947, subcontinental democracy has been continuously deepened, contested, and remade by citizens demanding that constitutional promises of liberty, equality, and fraternity be fulfilled in daily life.

Caste

Dalit Panthers & Mandal

The militant mobilization of the Dalit Panthers in Maharashtra (1972) and the nationwide implementation of the Mandal Commission report (1990) for Other Backward Classes reservations challenged hereditary social monopoly.

Gender

Hindu Code Bill & 2012 Delhi

From Dr. Ambedkar’s resignation over the Hindu Code Bills (1951) securing women’s inheritance rights to the nationwide mass uprising following the 2012 Delhi gang-rape, women continue to fight systemic violence and patriarchy.

Tribal

Forest Rights & Displacement

Decades of resistance against forced displacement by mega-dams (Narmada Bachao Andolan) and open-cast mining led to the landmark Forest Rights Act of 2006, recognizing ancestral adivasi self-governance.

Frontiers

Kashmir, Punjab & Northeast

The contested accession of Jammu and Kashmir, the traumatic agrarian and political crises of 1980s Punjab, and protracted struggles for ethnic self-determination and demilitarization across the Northeast test federal democracy.

Democracy

The Emergency (1975–1977)

When Prime Minister Indira Gandhi suspended fundamental constitutional rights and jailed political opponents, underground civil resistance and the subsequent 1977 elections reaffirmed the primacy of Indian democracy.

Living Connection

Freedom was not a destination. The struggles that began before 1947 — for caste, gender, tribal, and minority rights — continue today.
