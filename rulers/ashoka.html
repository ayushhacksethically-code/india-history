Ashoka the Great

Third Mauryan Emperor · Dhamma Patron · Ajivika Persecutor

In one line: The emperor who renounced conquest after Kalinga and spread moral edicts across South Asia — and whose reign also included documented persecution of Ajivikas and Jains.

Quick Facts

Reign c. 268 – 232 BCE (~36 years)
Dynasty Maurya Empire
Imperial Capital Pataliputra (modern Patna, Bihar)
Predecessor Bindusara (Father)
Successor Dasharatha Maurya (Grandson)
Key Contemporaries Antiochus II, Ptolemy II, Devanampiya Tissa

Life & Reign

Ashoka was the grandson of Chandragupta Maurya, who had forged the subcontinent's first vast empire with Chanakya, and the son of Emperor Bindusara. In his youth, Ashoka served as viceroy in the strategic commercial centers of Ujjain and Taxila, gaining early renown for suppressing provincial revolts. Upon Bindusara's death around 272 BCE, a violent four-year interregnum ensued. Later Buddhist chronicles, such as the Mahavamsa and the Ashokavadana, claim Ashoka assassinated ninety-nine of his brothers to secure the throne with the aid of the royal minister Radhagupta. While modern historians like Romila Thapar regard the figure of ninety-nine brothers as legendary magnification to dramatize his transformation from a ruthless prince (Chandashoka) into a righteous monarch (Dharmashoka), his own Pillar Edict V mentions his living brothers' households decades later, confirming that familial conflict was selectively mythologized.

Around 261 BCE, in the eighth year of his coronation, Ashoka initiated the military conquest of Kalinga (modern coastal Odisha), an independent maritime republic that resisted Mauryan subjugation. The war ended in catastrophic slaughter: Major Rock Edict XIII explicitly records that 100,000 individuals were slain, 150,000 were driven into captivity and deported, and countless others died from disease and starvation. The sight of the severed corpses and the grief of non-combatant ascetics, householders, and families struck the emperor with intense personal remorse, catalyzing his spiritual turn toward Buddhist moral teachings.

Rather than attempting further territorial conquests, Ashoka formulated the state philosophy of Dhamma (Dharma). Dhamma was not a sectarian religion imposed by force, but a civic and moral code emphasizing filial piety, generosity toward ascetics and Brahmins, non-injury to living creatures, and mutual concord among differing religious communities. Ashoka had his teachings engraved on monumental rock faces, cave walls, and polished sandstone pillars erected along public highways across modern India, Pakistan, Nepal, and Afghanistan, utilizing local scripts including Brahmi, Kharoshthi, Aramaic, and Greek.

To enforce this civic ethic, Ashoka created an innovative corps of administrative inspectors known as the Dhamma Mahamattas. These officials were dispatched to investigate public grievances, supervise the judicial treatment of prisoners, and promote charitable welfare. The imperial state funded public hospitals for both humans and animals, ordered the planting of shade-giving banyan trees and mango groves, and constructed wells and rest-houses at intervals along major commercial thoroughfares.

In his later reign, Ashoka sought to project moral influence across Asia and the Mediterranean basin. He patronized the Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra, presided over by monk Moggaliputta Tissa, which purged sectarian heresies from the Sangha and compiled the Kathavatthu. He dispatched diplomatic and religious envoys carrying medical herbs and ethical teachings to neighboring Hellenistic monarchs, specifically naming Antiochus II Theos of Syria, Ptolemy II Philadelphus of Egypt, Antigonus Gonatas of Macedonia, Magas of Cyrene, and Alexander of Epirus. To Sri Lanka, he sent his children Mahinda and Sanghamitta, who introduced the Theravada monastic lineage and carried a sapling of the original Bodhi Tree from Bodh Gaya.

Ashoka died around 232 BCE after a reign of roughly thirty-six years. Following his death, the immense centralized Mauryan administration rapidly fragmented under economic strain, vast provincial distances, and weak successors. Within fifty years of Ashoka's demise, the last Mauryan ruler, Brihadratha, was assassinated during an army review in 185 BCE by his commander-in-chief Pushyamitra Shunga, concluding the Maurya dynasty.

Documented Achievements

  • Renunciation of Imperial War: Publicly renounced aggressive military conquest following the Kalinga War, inscribing an unprecedented public confession of remorse in Rock Edict XIII.
  • Subcontinental Epigraphic Corpus: Inscribed over thirty major rock and pillar edicts across South Asia, creating the earliest deciphered historical inscriptions in Indian history.
  • Civic Welfare Infrastructure: Constructed an empire-wide network of public wells, rest-houses, shade groves, and botanical gardens providing medicinal herbs and veterinary hospitals for animals.
  • Dhamma Mahamattas: Established an oversight administrative corps charged with auditing prisons, alleviating wrongful punishments, and distributing charitable famine relief.
  • Third Buddhist Council: Convened and financed the landmark Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra (c. 250 BCE) to resolve ideological disputes within the Buddhist Sangha.
  • International Diplomacy: Dispatched peaceful embassies to Hellenistic Mediterranean kings and sent Mahinda and Sanghamitta to Sri Lanka, establishing global Buddhist ties.
  • Interfaith Concord: Promoted mutual respect among rival traditions, issuing Rock Edict XII which declared that honoring another person's sect strengthens one's own.

Documented Atrocities & Controversies

  • Devastation of Kalinga: Conducted a military campaign that resulted in 100,000 battle deaths and the forced deportation of 150,000 civilians into servitude (Rock Edict XIII).
  • Persecution of Ajivikas: The Ashokavadana (2nd c. CE Buddhist text) documents that Ashoka ordered the summary execution of 18,000 Ajivika followers at Pundravardhana following an anti-Buddhist desecration incident.
  • Sectarian Execution Orders: The same text records that he placed a bounty on Nirgrantha (Jain) heads in Pataliputra, which inadvertently led to the execution of his own brother Vitashoka.
  • Coercive Warnings to Forest Tribes: In Rock Edict XIII, he warned forest tribes (atavikas) that despite his remorse, the emperor still possessed the military power to punish and execute unrepentant populations.
  • Retention of Imperial Coercion: Modern historians (e.g. Upinder Singh, 2017) note that Ashoka renounced personal aggression but preserved the coercive military and taxation machinery of the Mauryan state.
  • Fratricidal Accession: Monastic chronicles record that he eliminated rival siblings to seize the throne, maintaining a palace prison known as 'Ashoka's Hell'.

Primary Evidence & Sources

Evidence — EB-A1: The Kalinga War & Epigraphic Remorse
  • Epigraphic: Major Rock Edict XIII (inscribed at Shahbazgarhi, Mansehra, Kalsi, Girnar, and Erragudi) contains the explicit statement: “One hundred and fifty thousand were deported, one hundred thousand were killed, and many times that number perished.”
  • Selective Omission: Notably, Rock Edict XIII was omitted from the rock faces at Dhauli and Jaugada in Kalinga itself, where separate edicts addressed Kalinga administrators without recounting the horrific body count.
  • Literary: Divyavadana and Mahavamsa record traditions of the Kalinga conquest and subsequent repentance.
  • Modern Scholarship: Thapar, Romila (1961, rev. 2002); Singh, Upinder (2017), Political Violence in Ancient India, Harvard University Press.
Limitations: The exact casualty figures in Rock Edict XIII represent ancient royal estimates, but archaeological excavations at Sisupalgarh (Kalinga) confirm defensive fortification breaches dating to the 3rd century BCE.
Evidence — EB-A2: The Ajivika & Jain Persecution Claims
  • Literary (Buddhist): The Sanskrit text Ashokavadana (preserved in the Chinese A-yu wang chuan, c. 300 CE) recounts that after a non-Buddhist at Pundravardhana drew a picture of the Buddha bowing to a Nirgrantha, Ashoka ordered 18,000 Ajivikas executed and placed a gold dinar bounty on sectarian heads.
  • Epigraphic Counter-Evidence: Barabar Cave inscriptions (Bihar) record Ashoka personally gifting beautifully polished rock-cut caves (Sudama and Karan Chaupar caves) to the Ajivika sect in the 12th and 19th years of his reign.
  • Modern Scholarship: Strong, John S. (1983), The Legend of King Aśoka; Basham, A.L. (1951), History and Doctrines of the Ājīvikas.
Limitations: The Ashokavadana is a later hagiography written centuries after Ashoka's death by Buddhist monks with strong sectarian biases; however, historians cite it to illustrate that ancient Buddhist memory did not remember Ashoka as an absolute pacifist.
Evidence — EB-A3: The Inscriptional Dhamma Corpus
  • Epigraphic Corpus: 14 Major Rock Edicts, 2 Separate Kalinga Edicts, 7 Pillar Edicts, and numerous Minor Rock and Pillar Inscriptions surviving across 30+ sites.
  • Multilingual Proof: Bilingual Greek-Aramaic inscription discovered at Kandahar (Afghanistan) demonstrating intentional translations of Buddhist ethical concepts into Hellenistic philosophical terms (translating Dhamma as Eusebeia).
  • Philological Standards: Corpus Inscriptionum Indicarum Vol. 1 (ed. E. Hultzsch, 1925); Norman, K.R. (2012), Aśokan Inscriptions, Pali Text Society.
Limitations: Inscriptions represent official imperial public self-presentation; they reveal royal policy and administrative intentions rather than unmediated daily practice across remote villages.
Evidence — EB-A4: The 99 Brothers Legend & Succession
  • Literary (Sri Lankan Chronicles): Dipavamsa (6.21–22) and Mahavamsa (5.19–20) assert that Ashoka slain 99 half-brothers, sparing only Tissa (Vitashoka).
  • Inscriptional Contradiction: Major Rock Edict V explicitly mentions the households and welfare of the king's brothers, sisters, and other relatives residing in Pataliputra and outlying towns in the 13th year of his coronation.
  • Modern Historiography: Lahiri, Nayanjot (2015), Ashoka in Ancient India; Thapar, Romila (2002).
Limitations: Monastic chronicles magnified his early depravity to highlight the miraculous transformative power of his conversion to Buddhism; the epigraphic evidence strongly suggests several siblings survived.

Key Chronology

Key Contemporaries

Chandragupta Maurya

Grandfather · Imperial Founder

Overthrew the Nandas and established the Maurya Empire with Chanakya; concluded his life via Jain asceticism at Shravanabelagola.

Bindusara ‘Amitraghata’

Father · Second Mauryan Emperor

Extended Mauryan rule into peninsular India; patronized the heterodox Ajivika sect and maintained diplomatic ties with Seleucid Syria.

Mahinda & Sanghamitta

Children · Monastic Missionaries

Led the historic Buddhist mission to Sri Lanka, ordaining the royal court of Anuradhapura and planting the Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi sapling.

Antiochus II Theos

Seleucid King (Syria & West Asia)

Named explicitly in Major Rock Edict XIII (Amtiyoko nama Yona-raja) as a recipient of Ashoka's diplomatic embassies and medicinal plant gifts.

Ptolemy II Philadelphus

Ptolemaic Pharaoh (Egypt)

Reigning Greek pharaoh of Alexandria named in Rock Edict XIII (Turamaye) who oversaw the Library of Alexandria and maritime trade links.

Devanampiya Tissa

King of Anuradhapura (Sri Lanka)

Allied monarch who adopted the imperial title Devanampiya ('Beloved of the Gods') and embraced Buddhism under the guidance of Prince Mahinda.

Historical Legacy

Ashoka's historical memory was largely forgotten in mainland India after the fall of the Mauryas until James Prinsep deciphered the Brahmi script in 1837, unlocking the rock inscriptions that revealed the royal title Devanampiya Piyadasi as Emperor Ashoka.

In 1947, independent India selected Ashoka's twenty-four-spoke wheel of righteousness—the Ashoka Chakra from the Sarnath pillar—for the center of the national tricolor flag, and adopted his Lion Capital of Sarnath as the official State Emblem of India, inscribing beneath it the Mundaka Upanishad motto Satyameva Jayate ('Truth Alone Triumphs').

In modern subcontinental historiography, Ashoka's legacy remains profoundly nuanced: celebrated internationally as a pioneering herald of ethical governance, inter-religious harmony, and state-sponsored public welfare, yet recognized by modern critical scholarship as an astute imperial monarch who retained state coercion, warned rebellious forest tribes, and presided over documented sectarian conflicts.
