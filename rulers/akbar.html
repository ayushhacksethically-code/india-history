Akbar the Great

Third Mughal Emperor · Sulh-i-kul Architect · Controversial Conqueror

In one line: The Mughal emperor who built a pluralistic court and ended jizya — and whose campaigns included documented massacres at Chittorgarh and Ranthambore.

Reign 1556 – 1605 CE (~49 years)
Dynasty Mughal Empire

Life & Reign

Jalal-ud-din Muhammad Akbar was born in October 1542 at the Rajput fortress of Amarkot (in modern Sindh), where his father, Emperor Humayun, had sought refuge during his wanderings following his defeat by Sher Shah Suri. Raised in the turbulent frontier courts of Kabul and Kandahar, Akbar grew up amidst military encampments and horse-riding rather than formal academic tutoring, remaining functionally illiterate throughout his life while developing an extraordinary memory and passion for philosophy, literature, and art.

In January 1556, following Humayun's accidental death in Delhi, the thirteen-year-old Akbar was crowned at Kalanaur in Punjab. Power was initially exercised by his military guardian and regent, Bairam Khan. That November, at the decisive Second Battle of Panipat, Mughal forces defeated the army of Hemu (Hemu Vikramaditya); Hemu was captured, wounded, and executed before Akbar. By 1560, growing restless under Bairam Khan's authoritarian regency, Akbar assumed direct imperial control, encouraging Bairam Khan to embark on a pilgrimage to Mecca during which the veteran general was assassinated in Gujarat.

The first two decades of Akbar's personal rule were characterized by aggressive military expansion across central and western India. In 1564, Mughal forces under Asaf Khan invaded the prosperous Gond tribal realm of Garha-Katanga, culminating in the death of Rani Durgavati and the annexation of her treasury. In 1568, Akbar besieged the premier Rajput bastion of Chittorgarh; after a protracted five-month resistance and the mass jauhar of Rajput noblewomen, Akbar ordered the storming of the fortress, followed by the recorded massacre of approximately 30,000 peasant defenders and townsfolk. Subsequent sieges of Ranthambore (1568), Gujarat (1572–73), and Bengal (1574–76) consolidated north Indian hegemony, while the hard-fought Battle of Haldighati (1576) initiated decades of guerilla war against Maharana Pratap of Mewar.

Concurrently, Akbar revolutionized Mughal statecraft through his celebrated Rajput alliance policy. Beginning with his marriage to Harkha Bai (daughter of Raja Bharmal of Amer) in 1562, Akbar integrated Rajput royal lineages into the highest echelons of the imperial aristocracy without requiring religious conversion. He appointed Raja Man Singh as his premier military commander and Raja Todar Mal as his chief revenue minister. In 1563, he abolished the tax on Hindu pilgrims, and in 1564, he repealed the discriminatory jizya poll-tax on non-Muslim subjects, establishing equal civic status for all subjects under imperial law.

In 1575, Akbar constructed the Ibadat Khana (House of Worship) at his newly built capital of Fatehpur Sikri, initiating structured theological debates among Sunni theologians. Frustrated by their factional intolerance, he expanded the assemblies to include Shia scholars, Hindu pandits, Jain acharyas (including Hiravijaya Suri), Parsi Zoroastrian priests, and Jesuit Catholic missionaries from Goa. In 1579, he issued the Mahzar (Infallibility Decree), claiming the supreme authority to choose between conflicting legal interpretations of Islamic law in the state's interest. He subsequently formulated the ethical doctrine of Sulh-i-Kul (universal peace and mutual tolerance) and founded the eclectic aristocratic spiritual order known as Tauhid-i-Ilahi (commonly referred to as Din-i-Ilahi) in 1582. On the administrative front, Raja Todar Mal designed the standardized Zabt and Dahsala revenue systems, which calculated agrarian taxes based on average ten-year crop yields and cash market prices.

Akbar's final years were shadowed by imperial exhaustion, court intrigue, and the rebellion of his eldest son, Prince Salim (later Emperor Jahangir), who declared independence at Allahabad and arranged the murder of Akbar's trusted friend and biographer, Abu'l-Fazl, in 1602. After an uneasy reconciliation with Salim, Akbar died of dysentery at Agra in October 1605 after a reign of forty-nine years. He was interred in a magnificent open-air mausoleum at Sikandra, leaving behind an integrated subcontinental empire of over one hundred million people.

Documented Achievements

  • Sulh-i-Kul Doctrine: Formulated and institutionalized the philosophical state doctrine of Sulh-i-Kul ('universal peace and absolute tolerance'), guaranteeing state neutrality toward differing religions.
  • Abolition of Jizya: Repealed the centuries-old discriminatory jizya poll-tax on non-Muslim subjects (1564) and eliminated the pilgrim tax on sacred Hindu pilgrimage centers (1563).
  • Rajput Integration: Forged durable political alliances with Hindu Rajput royal houses, appointing Raja Man Singh and Raja Todar Mal to the highest military and civil posts in the realm.
  • Ibadat Khana Debates: Established the world's first formal state-sponsored parliament of world religions at Fatehpur Sikri, hosting Muslim, Hindu, Jain, Parsi, and Christian theologians.
  • Zabti Revenue Modernization: Implemented Raja Todar Mal's revolutionary Dahsala system, establishing standardized land surveys, soil classifications, and fixed cash tax rates.
  • Mansabdari Bureaucracy: Created a meritocratic, unified civil-military administrative hierarchy (Mansabdari) that organized imperial officers by rank and cavalry quota.
  • Monumental Architecture & Translation: Constructed the planned city of Fatehpur Sikri and the grand Agra Fort, while commissioning landmark Persian translations of the Mahabharata (Razmnama) and Ramayana.

Documented Atrocities & Controversies

  • Chittorgarh Massacre (1568): Following the storming of Chittorgarh and the mass jauhar of Rajput women, ordered the documented slaughter of roughly 30,000 non-combatant peasants and townspeople (recorded in the Akbarnama).
  • Ranthambore Devastation: Waged brutal siege warfare against Rao Surjan Hada at Ranthambore (1568), using heavy artillery bombardments that devastated civilian quarters.
  • Gondwana Invasion (1564): Dispatched an unprovoked imperial campaign against the peaceful tribal realm of Garha-Katanga, resulting in the heroic suicide of Rani Durgavati and state plunder.
  • Exhausting War with Mewar: Waged prolonged warfare against Maharana Pratap following the Battle of Haldighati (1576), burning crops, capturing cattle, and devastating the Mewar peasantry.
  • Execution of Hemu (1556): Following the Second Battle of Panipat, permitted or participated in the summary beheading of the captured wounded commander Hemu, sending his head to Kabul.
  • Din-i-Ilahi Backlash: His spiritual initiative (Tauhid-i-Ilahi) provoked severe outrage among orthodox Muslim clerics, who viewed his court practices (such as prostration / sijdah) as blasphemy.
  • Court Panegyric Bias: Official histories written by Abu'l-Fazl functioned as court propaganda, glossing over widespread peasant tax rebellions and civilian suffering.

Primary Evidence & Sources

Evidence — EB-K1: The Siege of Chittorgarh (1568) & Civilian Casualties
  • Official Court Chronicle: Abu'l-Fazl, Akbarnama (Vol. II, Chapter 65) explicitly records: “When the fortress was taken, orders were given for a general slaughter... Nearly 30,000 persons were slain in the town and fort.”
  • Critical Contemporary Chronicle: Abdul Qadir Badauni, Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh, confirms the scale of the massacre and records that eight thousand Rajput warriors died in combat alongside thousands of peasant volunteers.
  • Rajput Oral & Epigraphic Memory: Contemporary Mewari ballads and subsequent inscriptions commemorate the third jauhar of Chittorgarh, where noblewomen entered sacred pyres led by Rani Phool Kanwar.
  • Modern Scholarship: Smith, Vincent A. (1917), Akbar the Great Mogul; Chandra, Satish (2007), Medieval India.
Limitations: While court chroniclers may have rounded the casualty number to 30,000 for dramatic imperial intimidation, the event is attested by both court panegyrists and independent contemporary critics.
Evidence — EB-K2: Sulh-i-Kul & The Abolition of Jizya
  • Official Administrative Code: Abu'l-Fazl, Ain-i-Akbari, describes the royal decree abolishing jizya in 1564, declaring that tax discrimination against subjects who worship God according to their own traditions is unjust.
  • European Jesuit Records: Letters of Father Antonio Monserrate (1580–1582) and Father Rodolfo Acquaviva, who observed Akbar's genuine fascination with diverse religious traditions, noting his deep defense of religious tolerance.
  • Imperial Firmans: Surviving Persian royal firmans granted to Jain leaders (such as Acharya Hiravijaya Suri) forbidding the slaughter of animals during the Paryushana festival throughout the empire.
  • Modern Scholarship: Habib, Irfan (1999), Akbar and His India, Oxford University Press; Moosvi, Shireen (2008).
Limitations: Historical records reveal that jizya was briefly reimposed in specific border provinces in 1575 before being permanently cancelled in 1579, showing that religious reform faced strong bureaucratic friction.
Evidence — EB-K3: Din-i-Ilahi & Orthodox Theological Backlash
  • Critical Orthodoxy Account: Badauni, Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh (Vol. II), bitterly attacks Akbar for allegedly abandoning Islam, introducing solar calendar worship, drinking water blessed by the sun, and commanding court nobles to perform sijdah (prostration).
  • Neutral Contemporary Persian Text: Dabistan-i-Mazahib (c. 1650s), an objective comparative treatise on Indian religions, describes the philosophical principles and eclectic vows of Akbar's order.
  • Modern Historiography: Rizvi, S.A.A. (1975), Religious and Intellectual History of the Muslims in Akbar's Reign; Mukhia, Harbans (2004), demonstrating that Din-i-Ilahi was an elite spiritual order (iradat) rather than an institutional new religion.
Limitations: Badauni wrote secretly out of personal and sectarian jealousy, often exaggerating court deviations; British colonial historians later misinterpreted the order as an act of personal megalomania.
Evidence — EB-K4: The Battle of Haldighati (1576) & Mewar Resistance
  • Contemporary Eye-Witness Account: Badauni participated in the Battle of Haldighati in person, recording that Akbar's commander Raja Man Singh led imperial Rajput and Mughal troops against Maharana Pratap's Afghan and Bhil warrior coalition.
  • Official Record: Abu'l-Fazl, Akbarnama (Vol. III), describes the ferocious battle in the mountain pass and acknowledges that Maharana Pratap escaped and continued resisting.
  • Rajput Bardic Texts: Rajprashasti Mahakavya (inscribed on Rajsamand Lake marble slabs) and Mewari bards document Pratap's refusal to accept personal Mughal court service.
  • Modern Historiography: Sarkar, Jadunath (1960), Military History of India; Sharma, G.N. (1954), Mewar and the Mughal Emperors.
Limitations: While modern popular memory often frames Haldighati as a purely religious Hindu-Muslim conflict, primary sources confirm that Akbar's vanguard was led by a Hindu Rajput (Man Singh), while Pratap's artillery and vanguard was commanded by an Afghan Muslim (Hakim Khan Sur).

Key Contemporaries

Maharana Pratap

King of Mewar · Unyielding Sovereign

Fierce Rajput monarch who refused submission to Mughal hegemony, waging lifelong guerrilla resistance across the Aravalli hills.

Rani Durgavati

Gondwana Queen · Forest Sovereign

Ruled Garha-Katanga with exceptional administrative wisdom, taking her own life in battle against invading Mughal forces in 1564.

Rani Chand Bibi

Warrior Queen of Ahmednagar

Heroically defended Ahmednagar Fort against Akbar's imperial army led by Prince Murad in 1595 before falling to court treachery.

Raja Todar Mal

Finance Minister · Revenue Architect

Genius administrative planner who created the landmark Zabti land survey and Dahsala taxation system that grounded the Mughal economy.

Abu'l-Fazl ibn Mubarak

Grand Vizier · Biographer

Author of the monumental Akbarnama and Ain-i-Akbari, court ideologue of Sulh-i-Kul, assassinated by Prince Salim's agents in 1602.

Queen Elizabeth I

Tudor Queen of England

Contemporary English monarch who chartered the British East India Company in 1600 and addressed formal diplomatic letters to Akbar.

Historical Legacy

Akbar's reign has long occupied a central position in the historical consciousness of South Asia. During the Indian anti-colonial national movement, leaders such as Jawaharlal Nehru celebrated Akbar as the premier historical embodiment of India's composite, secular, and pluralistic civilizational identity.

His architectural innovations—especially the synthesis of Persian, Gujarati, and Rajasthani stone-carving traditions in Fatehpur Sikri and Agra Fort—created an enduring subcontinental aesthetic. In popular culture, his affectionate partnership with courtier Birbal remains celebrated across generations in Hindi folk tales, while epics like K. Asif's 1960 cinematic classic Mughal-e-Azam and Ashutosh Gowariker's 2008 film Jodhaa Akbar cemented his cultural presence.

Simultaneously, his legacy is viewed with critical scrutiny: Rajput oral traditions preserve solemn memories of the catastrophic civilian slaughter at Chittorgarh, orthodox Islamic historiography remembers him as a disruptive theological innovator who undermined Sharia, and modern critical historians analyze him as a ruthless empire-builder who skillfully deployed inclusion and cultural patronage to legitimize centralized imperial dominance.
