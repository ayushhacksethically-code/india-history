CHAPTER 2 · c. 1500 BCE – 1750 CE

Astronomy & Observatories

From Vedic nakshatras to the Jantar Mantar

Plain summary: Two thousand years of astronomical observation — from calendar reckoning to mathematically sophisticated planetary models.

Quick Facts: Indian Astronomy

Earliest Evidence Vedic nakshatras (c. 1500 BCE)
Earliest Text Jyotisha Vedanga (c. 600 BCE)
Golden Age Aryabhata (499 CE), Varahamihira (6th c.)
Peak Observational Astronomy Jantar Mantar (18th c.)
Legacy Indian astronomy transmitted to Baghdad (8th c.)
Key Milestones 27 lunar mansions, axial rotation, epicyclic models, monumental stone yantras

1. Vedic Period: Nakshatras & Timekeeping

The earliest systematic celestial observations recorded in South Asia emerged within the Vedic liturgical corpus. The fundamental operational framework was the system of nakṣatras (“lunar mansions”), first catalogued sequentially in the Taittirīya Saṃhitā (4.4.10) and the Atharvaveda (19.7). The ecliptic was divided into 27 equal segments of 13°20′ each (or occasionally 28 segments incorporating the intercalary asterism Abhijit, Vega), corresponding approximately to the sidereal distance traversed by the Moon in a single solar day (27.32 days). Each nakshatra was identified by an asterism or junction star (yogatārā), such as Rohini (Aldebaran), Chitra (Spica), or Jyeshtha (Antares), providing an empirical celestial coordinate system against which to track planetary motion.

Vedic society operated an intricate lunisolar calendar. A pure lunar year of twelve synodic months (approximately 354.36 days) drifts against the tropical solar seasonal year of 365.24 days by roughly 11 days per annum. To prevent seasonal dislocation of pastoral and agricultural festivals, the Vedic calendar instituted an intercalary month (adhimāsa or mala-māsa) inserted approximately every thirty lunar months (or two extra months every five-year lustrum). The year was divided into six distinct two-month seasons (ṛtu): Vasanta (spring), Grishma (summer), Varsha (monsoon), Sharad (autumn), Hemanta (early winter), and Shishira (late winter).

The earliest dedicated astronomical manual in Indian literature is the Jyotiṣa Vedāṅga, traditionally attributed to the sage Lagadha and dated by modern philological consensus to approximately 600 BCE (with astronomical observational layers reflecting positions between 1400 and 1200 BCE). Preserved in two distinct recensions — one attached to the Ṛgveda (36 verses) and an expanded recension attached to the Yajurveda (43 verses) — the text provides arithmetic rules for computing the lengths of daytime and nighttime throughout the year, calculating the winter and summer solstices (uttarāyaṇa and dakṣiṇāyana), determining the new and full moon tithis, and calculating the Sun’s and Moon’s longitudinal entry into specific nakshatras.

The driving engine of this early astronomy was explicitly functional: the scheduling of sacred ritual sacrifices (yajña). Major royal rites such as the Aśvamedha and Rājasūya, as well as domestic seasonal soma rituals (Darśapūrṇamāsa), could only be initiated under auspicious celestial alignments, equinoxes, or specific lunar phases. Accurate observation of the heavens was deemed mandatory, as liturgical theology warned that sacrificial fires lit under mismatched astronomical coordinates would bring civic misfortune.

Evidence: Vedic Astronomical Texts and Calendar Treatises
  • Textual Corpus: Taittirīya Saṃhitā (4.4.10) listing 27 nakshatras beginning with Kṛttikā (Pleiades); Atharvaveda (19.7); Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa (2.1.2) noting that the Pleiades “do not swerve from the eastern quarter,” an alignment dating to c. 2300–2000 BCE.
  • Critical Editions: The Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa of Lagadha, critically edited with translation and commentary by K.S. Shukla (Indian National Science Academy, 1985); Pingree, David (1973), “The Mesopotamian Origin of Early Indian Mathematical Astronomy,” Journal for the History of Astronomy.
  • Modern Scholarly Analysis: Sarma, K.V. (1985), A Survey of Source Materials on Jyotisha in Sanskrit; Subbarayappa, B.V. and Sarma, K.V. (1985), Indian Astronomy: A Source-Book.
Limitations: Early dates (c. 1500–600 BCE) are based primarily on internal astronomical references to equinoctial alignments and linguistic philology. Because Vedic texts were transmitted entirely through oral recitation for centuries before being committed to physical writing, textual interpolations and redactions cannot be completely disentangled.

2. Aryabhata and the Classical Period (5th–6th c. CE)

The transition from rule-of-thumb calendar arithmetic to rigorous mathematical astronomy was inaugurated by Āryabhaṭa (476–550 CE). Working in Kusumapura (ancient Pataliputra, modern Patna) during the zenith of the Gupta empire, Aryabhata completed his magnum opus, the Āryabhaṭīya, in 499 CE at the stated age of twenty-three. Composed in 121 compact Sanskrit verses divided into four sections (pādas), the treatise revolutionized South Asian spherical astronomy (Gola) and computation (Gaṇita).

In the opening astronomical section, the Daśagītika (“Ten Stanzas in the Gītī Metre”), Aryabhata set forth astronomical parameters using an ingenious alphanumeric coding system (akṣara-saṃkhyā), compressing complex orbital durations, planetary revolutions per mahāyuga (4,320,000 years), and orbital inclinations into easily memorable verse. In the concluding Golapāda (verse 9), Aryabhata formulated his most revolutionary hypothesis: the diurnal rotation of the Earth on its axis. Explaining the apparent westward transit of constellations, he deployed his famous nautical analogy:

“Just as a man in a boat moving forward sees stationary objects on the bank moving backward, even so an observer at Lanka [the equator] sees the stationary stars moving directly toward the west.” — Āryabhaṭīya, Golapāda, Verse 9 (trans. W.E. Clark, 1930)

Aryabhata’s assertion of a spinning Earth was deeply controversial among contemporary and later Indian astronomers. Conservators of orthodox geostatic cosmological frameworks — most notably Varahamihira, Brahmagupta, and Lalla — vehemently rejected terrestrial rotation, arguing that if the Earth spun, birds taking flight would never be able to return to their nests and soaring masonry towers would collapse from continuous inertial drift.

Aryabhata also discarded mythological explanations for celestial phenomena. In Golapāda (verses 37–48), he demonstrated that lunar and solar eclipses are produced strictly by optical shadows: a lunar eclipse occurs when the Moon enters the dark shadow cone cast by the Earth (bhū-cchāyā), while a solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes directly between the Earth and the Sun, obscuring the solar disk. He provided trigonometric geometric formulas to calculate the exact timing, duration, and magnitude of totality, explicitly refuting the mythological tale of the severed demon heads Rahu and Ketu swallowing the luminaries.

To calculate non-uniform planetary motions, Aryabhata developed an elaborate epicyclic planetary model consisting of two concentric geometric corrections: the manda correction (an eccentric/epicycle circle accounting for Keplerian elliptical velocity variation) and the śīghra correction (an epicycle circle accounting for the optical heliocentric projection of the Earth’s and planets’ mutual orbits). These computations rested upon his table of 24 sine differences (jyā), constructed in steps of 3°45′ across a quadrant of radius $R = 3438$ minutes.

Evidence: Aryabhata's Textual Corpus and Mathematical Treatises
  • Primary Manuscript: Āryabhaṭīya, surviving in numerous palm-leaf manuscripts across Kerala and Tamil Nadu with classical commentaries by Bhaskara I (Āryabhaṭīyabhāṣya, 629 CE) and Nilakantha Somayaji (Bhāṣya, c. 1500 CE).
  • Critical Translation: Clark, Walter Eugene (trans.) (1930), The Āryabhaṭīya of Āryabhaṭa: An Ancient Indian Work on Mathematics and Astronomy, University of Chicago Press; Shukla, K.S. (ed. & trans.) (1976), Aryabhatiya of Aryabhata, Indian National Science Academy.
  • Modern Scholarly Analysis: Pingree, David (1978), “History of Mathematical Astronomy in India,” Dictionary of Scientific Biography, Vol. 15; Plofker, Kim (2009), Mathematics in India, Princeton University Press.
Limitations: The historical transmission debate between Greek and Indian planetary kinematics remains intensely contested. While Aryabhata’s epicyclic parameters differ substantially from Ptolemy’s Almagest, similarities with pre-Ptolemaic Hellenistic astronomy indicate cross-cultural conceptual diffusion whose exact historical channels remain incompletely documented.

3. Varahamihira and the Pancasiddhantika (6th c. CE)

In the mid-sixth century, the city of Ujjain in central India (ancient Avanti) solidified its position as the premier astronomical hub of South Asia. At the court of the Aulikara or Gupta kings flourished Varāhamihira (c. 505–587 CE), an astronomer and encyclopedic polymath. His seminal astronomical contribution was the Pañcasiddhāntikā (“Compendium of Five Astronomical Treatises”), composed c. 575 CE. Recognizing that earlier manuals had fallen into observational error or contained divergent methodologies, Varahamihira systematically reviewed, compared, and mathematically reconciled the five leading astronomical schools of his era:

  1. Paitāmaha Siddhānta: The most archaic system, retaining the simple five-year yuga lunisolar parameters of the Jyotiṣa Vedāṅga, which Varahamihira dismissed as obsolete and hopelessly inaccurate.
  2. Vāsiṣṭha Siddhānta: A transitional treatise incorporating non-uniform solar and lunar motions using zig-zag functions analogous to Babylonian lunar tables.
  3. Pauliśa Siddhānta: A mathematically advanced treatise featuring geometric calculations for solar and lunar eclipses, commonly linked by historians to Hellenistic origins (possibly derived from Paul of Alexandria, c. 378 CE).
  4. Romaka Siddhānta: Explicitly named “The Roman Treatise,” this school utilized the Metonic cycle of 19 tropical solar years (235 synodic lunar months) and a tropical year length of 365 days, 5 hours, 55 minutes, and 12 seconds, mirroring the parameters of Hipparchus.
  5. Sūrya Siddhānta: Judged by Varahamihira to be the most accurate and authoritative of the five, preserving sophisticated epicyclic parameters and planetary coordinate tables that became the foundation of all later classical Siddhantic astronomy.

Varahamihira’s work is especially celebrated for its candid acknowledgment of foreign scientific achievement. In a well-known passage in his encyclopedic Bṛhat Saṃhitā (2.15), he penned an impassioned defense of scientific inquiry across cultural boundaries:

“The Greeks (Yavanas), indeed, are foreigners (mlecchas), but with them this science (astronomy) is in a flourishing state. Therefore, they are honored even like sages; how much more then should an astronomer who is a twice-born be honored?” — Bṛhat Saṃhitā, Chapter 2, Verse 15 (trans. M. Ramakrishna Bhat, 1981)

Beyond technical planetary kinematics, Varahamihira’s Bṛhat Saṃhitā represented an encyclopedic survey of physical sciences, containing 106 chapters covering meteorology, cloud classification, seasonal rainfall prediction, groundwater hydrology (locating subterranean aquifers via botanical indicator species and termite mounds), gemology, architecture (Vāstuśāstra), and botanical agronomy.

Evidence: Pancasiddhantika and Classical Astrological Manuscripts
  • Primary Manuscript: Unique surviving manuscripts of the Pañcasiddhāntikā recovered in Gujarat and Benares; critical edition and translation by George Thibaut and Mahamahopadhyaya Sudhakara Dvivedi (1889).
  • Encyclopedic Edition: Varāhamihira's Bṛhat Saṃhitā, edited with English translation by M. Ramakrishna Bhat (Motilal Banarsidass, 1981).
  • Modern Scholarly Analysis: Neugebauer, Otto and Pingree, David (1970–1971), The Pañcasiddhāntikā of Varāhamihira, Det Kongelige Danske Videnskabernes Selskab; van der Waerden, B.L. (1988), “The Romaka Siddhānta,” Archive for History of Exact Sciences.
Limitations: The exact transmission route from Roman Alexandria or Greco-Bactrian centers to northwest India (via the Romaka and Paulisha schools) is debated. While linguistic borrowings (such as Sanskrit kendra from Greek kentron, and jāmitra from diametron) confirm historical contact, the identity of the translators and the intermediary texts remain unrecovered archaeologically.

4. Brahmagupta & Bhaskara II: Mathematical Astronomy

In the seventh century, mathematical astronomy reached unprecedented computational precision in Rajasthan through the work of Brahmagupta (598–668 CE), court astronomer to King Vyaghramukha of the Chapa dynasty at Bhillamala (modern Bhinmal). In 628 CE, Brahmagupta composed his masterwork, the Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (“The Corrected Treatise of Brahma”), spanning 24 chapters. In it, he systematically improved algorithms for computing true planetary longitudes, the visual conjunction of planets with stars (bha-graha-yuti), the exact duration and phases of lunar and solar eclipses, and the precise moment of lunar crescent visibility (śṛṅgonnati).

Five centuries later, classical Siddhantic astronomy culminated in the work of Bhāskara II (Bhāskarācārya, 1114–1185 CE), who directed the observatory at Ujjain. In 1150 CE, Bhaskara completed the Siddhānta Śiromaṇi (“Crown Jewel of Treatises”), a four-part mathematical and astronomical compendium comprising the Līlāvatī (arithmetic), Bījagaṇita (algebra), Grahagaṇitādhyāya (planetary mathematics), and Golādhyāya (spherical geometry and astronomy).

Bhaskara II made profound theoretical breakthroughs in mathematical mechanics. To compute the instantaneous daily velocity of planets, he introduced the concept of tātkālikī gati (“instantaneous velocity”). Recognizing that average motion over twenty-four hours fails to capture the true kinematic state of a planet at a specific instant, he evaluated infinitesimal time differences ($\delta t$), formulating what modern mathematicians recognize as an early precursor to differential calculus:

$$\delta(\sin heta) = \cos heta \cdot \delta heta$$ — Mathematical equivalence of Bhaskara II's differential rule for planetary sine variations

Despite these extraordinary mathematical innovations, Indian Siddhantic astronomy remained firmly embedded in a geocentric paradigm. In the Siddhantic universe, the spherical Earth was stationary at the gravitational center of concentric celestial planetary spheres. While epicyclic geometries accurately reproduced the visible longitudinal paths, retrogradations, and stations of Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Venus, and Mercury, Indian astronomers did not develop a physical heliocentric dynamical mechanics. Even when Aryabhata postulated axial terrestrial spin, the global community of Siddhantic astronomers adhered to a geocentric cosmological architecture until the introduction of European Copernican astronomy in the eighteenth century.

Evidence: Treatises of Brahmagupta and Bhaskara II
  • Textual Editions: Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta, critically edited by Ram Swarup Sharma (Indian Institute of Astronomical and Sanskrit Research, 1966); Siddhānta Śiromaṇi, edited with Vāsanābhāṣya by Bapudev Sastri (Benares, 1866).
  • Historical Translations: Colebrooke, Henry Thomas (1817), Algebra, with Arithmetic and Mensuration, from the Sanscrit of Brahmegupta and Bhascara, London: John Murray; Wilkinson, L. and Sastri, Bapudev (1861), The Siddhanta Siromani, Calcutta: C.B. Lewis.
  • Modern Scholarly Analysis: Pingree, David (1978), “History of Mathematical Astronomy in India”; Plofker, Kim (2009), Mathematics in India; Joseph, George Gheverghese (2011), The Crest of the Peacock.
Limitations: The geocentric framework limited computational predictions for planetary distances and orbital physical mechanics. While empirical accuracy for eclipses and conjunctions was high, no physical theory of gravitation or heliocentric orbital mechanics was developed within the classical Siddhantic tradition.

5. Observational Instruments & Yantras

Astronomical theory in ancient and medieval India was continually calibrated against observational instrumentation known collectively as yantras. Treatise chapters titled Yantrādhyāya (“Chapter on Instruments”) appear in the works of Aryabhata, Lalla (8th c.), King Bhoja of Dhara (11th c.), and Bhaskara II, detailing specialized devices constructed from wood, brass, ivory, and masonry:

  • The Gnomon (Śaṅku): A vertical cylindrical or conical rod of standardized height (typically 12 aṅgulas or finger-breadths) mounted upon a perfectly leveled horizontal masonry platform. By measuring the length and angle of the gnomon’s shadow cast by the sun, astronomers determined the exact cardinal directions (via intersecting circles drawn at morning and evening shadow tips), local geographical latitude, true solar noon, equinoxes, and summer/winter solstices.
  • Water Clocks (Ghaṭikā Yantra or Clepsydra): Timekeeping throughout day and night relied on sinking-bowl water clocks. A calibrated hemispherical copper bowl with a microscopic perforation in its apex was placed on the surface of a tranquil water basin; water slowly seeped into the bowl until it sank precisely at the expiration of one ghaṭikā (24 minutes, or 60 nadis in a sidereal day). Attendants marked the sinking by striking a bronze gong (ghaṇṭā).
  • The Armillary Sphere (Gola Yantra): A complex nested assembly of concentric wooden or brass rings representing the celestial equator, ecliptic, meridian, horizon, and solstitial colures, used to demonstrate celestial coordinates and teach students spherical trigonometry.
  • The Sighting Tube and Arc Instruments: Specialized graduated instruments included the Turīya yantra (quadrant), Dhanur yantra (semicircular arc), Cakra yantra (full 360-degree graduated circle), and the Phalaka yantra, an equatorial board instrument invented by Bhaskara II equipped with a sighting peg and plumb line for rapidly converting solar altitude into local time.

Pre-modern observational research was organized at designated scholarly centers across the subcontinent. Foremost among these was Ujjain, considered the prime meridian ($0^\circ$ longitude) of ancient Indian geography — analogous to modern Greenwich — as well as the Tropic of Cancer. Other continuous observational schools operated at Varanasi on the Ganges, Dhar in Malwa, and Valabhi in Saurashtra.

Evidence: Sanskrit Instrument Texts and Extant Artifacts
  • Textual Chapters: Siddhānta Śiromaṇi (Yantrādhyāya, Chapter 11) describing 15 distinct instruments; King Bhoja of Dhara’s Rājamṛgāṅka (c. 1042 CE); Padmanābha’s Yantraratnāvalī (c. 1400 CE).
  • Archaeological Instruments: Medieval graduated brass astrolabes (such as the 16th-century instruments produced by the famous astrolabe-makers of Lahore, preserved in the Red Fort Museum, Delhi); stone sighting pillars at ancient Ujjain.
  • Modern Scholarly Analysis: Rao, S. Balachandra (2005), Indian Astronomy: An Introduction; Sarma, Sreeramula Rajeswara (2019), A Descriptive Catalogue of Indian Astronomical Instruments.
Limitations: Because the vast majority of classical pre-18th-century observational instruments were fashioned from wood, cloth, or melted-down copper and brass alloys, direct physical specimens prior to the 16th century have largely decomposed or been lost to history. Historians must reconstruct their exact dimensions, calibration margins, and operating errors primarily from manuscript descriptions.

6. Jantar Mantar: Jai Singh II's Observatories (18th c.)

The zenith of monumental naked-eye observational astronomy in India occurred in the early eighteenth century through the visionary patronage of Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II of Amber and Jaipur (1688–1743). A high-ranking statesman in the Mughal imperial administration under Emperor Muhammad Shah, Jai Singh was an ardent student of both classical Sanskrit Siddhantas and Persian-Islamic astronomy (notably Ulugh Beg’s fifteenth-century observatory at Samarkand).

Observing substantial discrepancies between calculated planetary positions and observed celestial transits — which led to errors in calendar reckoning and astrological tables — Jai Singh initially experimented with standard portable brass astrolabes and armillary spheres. However, he concluded that small metal instruments suffered from inevitable structural defects: bearing wear, axes that flexed under thermal expansion, vibrations in windy conditions, and graduation scales too minute to read arc-seconds. In his Persian astronomical preface to the Zīj-i Muḥammad Shāhī (1728), Jai Singh articulated his bold architectural solution:

“Therefore, he constructed here of stone and lime, of perfect stability and strength, instruments of his own invention... so that the shaking of the instruments, the wear of the bearings, and the displacement of the centers were entirely removed.” — Sawai Jai Singh II, Preface to the Zīj-i Muḥammad Shāhī (1728)

Between 1724 and 1738, Jai Singh constructed five monumental stone observatories, known colloquially as Jantar Mantar (derived from Sanskrit yantra-mantra, “instruments and formulas”):

  1. Delhi (1724): Constructed outside the walled city of Shahjahanabad, featuring the massive Samrat Yantra, Jai Prakash, and Ram Yantra.
  2. Jaipur (1728–1734): The largest, most complex, and best preserved of the five complexes, erected adjacent to the City Palace. Inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2010.
  3. Ujjain (1734): Erected at the historic prime meridian, containing smaller masonry quadrants and dials.
  4. Varanasi (1737): Built atop the fortified roof of the Man Mandir Ghat overlooking the sacred Ganges.
  5. Mathura (1738): Constructed within the ancient Mughal fort, now largely dismantled and lost.

The masonry instruments built at Jaipur represent an astounding convergence of architectural geometry and optical precision. The Bṛhat Samrāt Yantra (“Supreme Instrument”) is the world’s largest stone sundial: its towering triangular gnomon stands 27 meters (90 feet) high, oriented precisely to the celestial North Pole at an angle corresponding to Jaipur’s latitude ($26^\circ 55^\prime$ N). Flanked by two colossal curved marble quadrant scales with a radius of 15 meters, the gnomon’s shadow moves at a visible speed of approximately 4 millimeters per minute (1 millimeter every 15 seconds), allowing observers to tell local solar time to an accuracy of two seconds.

Equally revolutionary was the Jai Prakāsh Yantra, an instrument of Jai Singh’s own invention. Consisting of two complementary sunken hemispherical marble bowls set into the ground, its interior surface is engraved with coordinate lines reflecting the celestial sphere inverted. Taut crosshairs stretched across the rim cast a shadow into the bowl, allowing an observer standing inside on narrow passageways to read altitude, azimuth, hour angle, and declination of stars directly against celestial markings.

Jai Singh was fully aware of contemporary European astronomy. He dispatched intellectual missions to Lisbon and Rome, invited Jesuit astronomer-mathematicians (Father Manuel de Figueiredo, Father Claude Boudier, and Anton Gabelsberger) to Jaipur, and acquired Philippe de La Hire’s Tabulae Astronomicae and John Flamsteed’s catalogs. Yet, despite possessing small European refracting telescopes, Jai Singh made the deliberate decision to base his primary observational program on giant naked-eye masonry monuments — a brilliant culmination of the medieval astrometric tradition just as European astronomy was pivoting decisively to telescopic physics.

Evidence: Archaeological Complexes and Royal Astronomical Archives
  • UNESCO World Heritage Inscription: The Jantar Mantar, Jaipur, inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage Site No. 1338 (2010), recognizing its monumental stone masonry and outstanding universal value in the history of science.
  • Primary Manuscripts: Zīj-i Muḥammad Shāhī (Persian manuscript, 1728); Jagannatha Samrat’s Siddhānta-samrāṭ (Sanskrit translation and expansion of Ptolemy’s Almagest); royal diplomatic correspondences with Jesuit astronomers preserved in the Jaipur City Palace Archives (Pothikhana).
  • Modern Scholarly Analysis: Sharma, Virendra Nath (1995), Sawai Jai Singh and His Astronomy, Motilal Banarsidass; Volwahsen, Andreas (2001, 2005), Cosmic Architecture in India: The Astronomical Observatories of Maharaja Jai Singh II, Prestel.
Limitations: The relative influence of European versus Indo-Persian and traditional Sanskrit methods in Jai Singh’s thought remains contested. While Jesuit astronomers provided European ephemerides, Jai Singh retained a Ptolemaic geocentric framework and chose not to adopt telescopic micrometers, leaving his observatories at the boundary between medieval naked-eye grandeur and modern telescopic astronomy.

7. Transmission to the Islamic World

In the late eighth century, the scientific traditions of India became a foundational pillar in the rapid flourishing of early Islamic science during the Abbasid Caliphate. Following the foundation of Baghdad as the new imperial capital in 762 CE, Caliph Abu Ja‘far al-Mansur established a policy of actively patronizing scientific translations from Persian, Syriac, Greek, and Sanskrit.

According to the historical chronicle of Ibn al-Adami (Niẓām al-‘Iqd) preserved in Ibn al-Qifti’s Ta’rīkh al-Ḥukamā’ (“History of Learned Men”), an embassy from Sindh arrived at the Abbasid court in 154 AH (771/773 CE) accompanied by an Indian astronomical scholar (named in Arabic sources as Kankah or Mankah). This scholar presented Caliph Al-Mansur with Sanskrit astronomical texts, most prominently Brahmagupta’s Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta and Khaṇḍakhādyaka.

Deeply impressed by the mathematical precision of the tables, Al-Mansur commissioned court astronomers Muhammad ibn Ibrahim al-Fazari and Ya‘qub ibn Tariq to translate the texts into Arabic with the scholar’s guidance. The resulting treatise, titled the Zīj al-Sindhind (an Arabic rendering of the Sanskrit Siddhānta), became the premier astronomical manual across the Islamic world for over half a century. Brahmagupta’s Khaṇḍakhādyaka was simultaneously translated as the Zīj al-Arkand.

These translations transmitted vital mathematical tools to the Islamic world: the decimal positional number system with zero (al-arqām al-hindiyya), the mathematical trigonometric functions of sine (al-jayb, from Sanskrit jyā) and versine (jayb al-tankīs), and algorithms for solar and lunar eclipse prediction. Later in the ninth century, Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi composed his own celebrated astronomical tables, Zīj al-Sindhind, which systematically synthesized Indian planetary methods with Ptolemaic spherical geometry.

The most profound and critical Islamic engagement with Indian astronomy was authored by the Persian polymath Abū Rayḥān al-Bīrūnī (973–1048 CE). Accompanying Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni on military campaigns into northern India, Al-Biruni spent years in the Punjab, mastered Sanskrit, read classical treatises by Aryabhata, Varahamihira, and Brahmagupta in the original, and collaborated with Brahmin pandits. In his magnum opus, Taḥqīq mā li-l-Hind (“Verification of what is said about India,” known in the West as Indica, 1030 CE), Al-Biruni provided a rigorous, objective, and intellectually sympathetic analysis of Indian astronomy. He praised Indian trigonometric ingenuity and Aryabhata’s hypothesis of Earth’s rotation, while offering scholarly criticism of how conservative astronomers compromised empirical mathematics to placate orthodox theological taboos.

Evidence: Arabic Historical Chronicles and Astronomical Translations
  • Arabic Primary Sources: Ibn al-Nadim’s Kitāb al-Fihrist (c. 987 CE); Ibn al-Qifti’s Ta’rīkh al-Ḥukamā’ (c. 1248 CE); surviving fragments of Al-Fazari and Ya‘qub ibn Tariq compiled by David Pingree.
  • Al-Biruni's Masterwork: Sachau, Edward C. (trans.) (1888, 1910), Alberuni's India: An Account of the Religion, Philosophy, Literature, Geography, Chronology, Astronomy, Customs, Laws and Astrology of India about A.D. 1030, 2 vols., London: Kegan Paul, Trench, Trübner & Co.
  • Modern Scholarly Analysis: Pingree, David (1970), “The Fragments of the Works of al-Fazārī,” Journal of Near Eastern Studies; Kennedy, E.S. (1956), A Survey of Islamic Astronomical Tables, American Philosophical Society; Saliba, George (2007), Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance, MIT Press.
Limitations: The original Arabic manuscripts of the 8th-century translations by Al-Fazari and Ya‘qub ibn Tariq have not survived intact; their parameters, tables, and text must be reconstructed retrospectively from citations in later 10th- to 12th-century Andalusian and Persian commentaries. The exact personal identity of the Indian scholars at the Abbasid court remains shrouded in transliteration variants.

8. Transmission to Europe & Modern Legacy

The intellectual journey of Indian astronomy and mathematics continued westward into medieval and Renaissance Europe via Islamic Spain (Al-Andalus) and Norman Sicily. In twelfth-century Toledo and Cordoba, Christian, Jewish, and Muslim scholars translated Arabic scientific treatises into Latin. Through this conduit, Adelard of Bath translated Al-Khwarizmi’s Zīj al-Sindhind into Latin in 1126 CE, introducing European scholars to the Indian-derived sine function. The Sanskrit term jīvā (chord/sine), phoneticized into Arabic as jayb (“pocket” or “fold”), was translated literally into Latin as sinus, giving birth to the modern mathematical term “sine.”

In 1202 CE, Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci) published his groundbreaking manual Liber Abaci, declaring that the nine Indian digits and the symbol zero (zephirum) represented the Modus Indorum (“method of the Indians”), an arithmetic system vastly superior to Roman numerals for astronomical calculation, navigation, and commerce.

The Kerala School and the Transmission Debate: As discussed in detail in Chapter 1 (Mathematics & Number Systems), astronomers of the Kerala School (founded by Madhava of Sangamagrama, c. 1340–1425) made extraordinary advances in mathematical astronomy. In 1501 CE, Nīlakaṇṭha Somayājī composed his astronomical masterpiece, the Tantrasaṅgraha, followed by his Āryabhaṭīyabhāṣya, in which he formulated a revolutionary geo-heliocentric planetary model: the five visible planets (Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn) move in heliocentric orbits around the Sun, while the Sun itself revolves around a stationary central Earth. This model mathematically anticipated the famous planetary system proposed in 1588 by Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe.

A major historiographical controversy persists regarding whether Jesuit missionaries operating in Cochin and Calicut in the sixteenth century transmitted Kerala astronomical tables and infinite series back to Europe (advocated by C.K. Raju and George Gheverghese Joseph), or whether European mathematicians independently rediscovered these series in the seventeenth century (the consensus held by Kim Plofker, David Pingree, and Dhruv Raina). To date, no physical translation or direct citation of a Kerala palm-leaf manuscript has been found in European Jesuit archives.

Modern Indian Astronomy and Space Science: In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Indian astronomy transitioned from traditional Siddhantic astrology to modern professional astrophysics. Milestone institutions included the Madras Observatory (established 1792 by the British East India Company, which discovered asteroids and produced major star catalogs), the Kodaikanal Solar Observatory (established 1899, pioneer of the Evershed effect in sunspots), and the Indian Astronomical Observatory at Hanle in Ladakh, which at 4,500 meters (14,764 ft) elevation is one of the highest optical, infrared, and gamma-ray observatories in the world.

Today, Indian astronomy is led by the Indian Space Research Organisation (ISRO) and premier research institutes like the Inter-University Centre for Astronomy and Astrophysics (IUCAA) and the Indian Institute of Astrophysics (IIA). Landmarks include AstroSat (launched in 2015 as India’s first dedicated multi-wavelength space telescope, observing in ultraviolet, optical, and X-ray bands), the Chandrayaan lunar exploration program, and the Aditya-L1 solar observatory (inserted into halo orbit around the Sun-Earth L1 Lagrange point in January 2024 to study the solar corona, coronal mass ejections, and space weather).

Evidence: Historiographical Transmission and Modern Astrophysics
  • Latin Manuscripts: Adelard of Bath’s 12th-century Latin translation of Al-Khwarizmi’s Zīj; Fibonacci’s Liber Abaci (1202, Biblioteca Nazionale di Firenze).
  • Kerala Geo-heliocentric Model: Nilakantha Somayaji’s Tantrasaṅgraha (1501 CE), critically edited with translation by K. Ramasubramanian and M.S. Sriram (Springer, 2011).
  • Modern Scientific Repositories: Royal Astronomical Society archives for Madras and Kodaikanal observatories; ISRO Science Data Management repositories documenting AstroSat and Aditya-L1 observations.
  • Modern Scholarly Analysis: Plofker, Kim (2009), Mathematics in India; Saliba, George (2007), Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance; Kochhar, Rajesh and Narlikar, Jayant (1995), Astronomy in India: A Historical Perspective.
Limitations: Direct material evidence connecting Indian Siddhantic or Kerala astronomical manuscripts to early modern European astronomers remains unverified; historians treat parallel independent development under similar trigonometric and navigational imperatives as the established academic consensus.

9. How Do We Know? Evidence Profiles in Indian Astronomy

Reconstructing two millennia of Indian astronomical history requires synthesizing four distinct evidentiary categories, each possessing unique methodological strengths and severe interpretive limitations:

EVIDENCE STREAM 1: PRIMARY TEXTUAL MANUSCRIPTS

Sanskrit Treatises and Arabic-Latin Translations

The most abundant and mathematically rigorous evidence base consists of surviving Sanskrit astronomical treatises (siddhāntas, karaṇas, and koṣṭhakas) preserved on palm-leaf and birch-bark manuscripts in archives such as the Saraswathi Mahal Library in Thanjavur, the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute in Pune, and European university libraries. These are complemented by eighth- to tenth-century Arabic translations (such as the Sindhind corpus) and eleventh-century critical accounts by Al-Biruni, which frequently preserve earlier computational variants whose Indian originals have been lost.

EVIDENCE STREAM 2: ARCHAEOLOGICAL INSTRUMENTS

Monumental Masonry and Metal Artifacts

Unlike textual manuscripts that can be altered during scribal recopying, archaeological instruments provide direct physical proof of observational capability. The eighteen-century masonry observatories of Sawai Jai Singh II at Jaipur, Delhi, Ujjain, and Varanasi remain intact as monumental calibration laboratories. In contrast, pre-eighteenth-century physical instruments are exceptionally scarce: surviving artifacts are limited to medieval brass astrolabes, armillary spheres, and celestial globes (such as the seamless celestial globes produced by Mughal metallurgists in Lahore using the lost-wax casting technique).

EVIDENCE STREAM 3: EPIGRAPHIC DATING RECORDS

Inscriptions, Copper Plates, and Eclipse Calendars

Over 100,000 epigraphic inscriptions and royal copper-plate charters across the subcontinent provide an independent chronological cross-check. Because royal grants (śāsanas) were traditionally formalized during auspicious astronomical events — specifically solar eclipses, lunar eclipses, equinoxes, and planetary conjunctions — scribes meticulously recorded the exact tithi, nakshatra, and weekday. Modern historians use high-precision retro-calculations from modern celestial mechanics (such as NASA ephemeris datasets) to verify the historical veracity and exact Gregorian calendar date of these ancient records.

EVIDENCE STREAM 4: MODERN COMPUTATIONAL RECONSTRUCTION

Algorithmic Testing of Ancient Planetary Parameters

Since the pioneering work of David Pingree and modern historians of exact sciences, researchers have converted ancient Sanskrit algorithms into computer software. By running ancient planetary formulas against known celestial events across centuries, scholars can evaluate the exact error margins of Aryabhata, Brahmagupta, or Jai Singh, identifying whether discrepancies stemmed from systemic mathematical models, geographical refraction errors, or naked-eye sighting limitations.

Divergent Evidence Profiles across Historical Domains: The history of Indian astronomy exhibits sharp evidentiary imbalances across sub-fields. While mathematical and computational astronomy is supported by an extraordinarily dense and continuous textual corpus spanning 1,500 years, the physical history of observational instruments suffers from acute material scarcity prior to 1700 CE due to climate and perishable construction materials. Simultaneously, cross-cultural transmission (whether eastward from Greece or westward to the Islamic world and Europe) remains the most intensely debated frontier, where direct manuscript transmission chains are rare and historians must carefully weigh circumstantial linguistic, algorithmic, and parameter parallels.

Evidence: Methodological Synthesis of Astrometric History
  • Epigraphic Verification Studies: Kielhorn, Franz (1888–1900), “Synchronisms of the Inscriptions of Southern and Northern India,” Epigraphia Indica; Sircar, D.C. (1965), Indian Epigraphy.
  • Computational History: Pingree, David (1978), History of Mathematical Astronomy in India; Yano, Michio (1986), “The Karaṇaratna of Deva,” Journal of Indian and Buddhist Studies; Plofker, Kim (2009), Mathematics in India.
  • Historiographical Catalogues: Pingree, David (1970–1994), Census of the Exact Sciences in Sanskrit (CESS), Series A, Vols. 1–5, American Philosophical Society.
Limitations: Computational retro-analysis demonstrates mathematical capability, but cannot determine whether a given ancient astronomer physically observed an event or merely calculated it as a theoretical exercise. Furthermore, loss of regional archives due to warfare, humidity, and temple destructions has created geographical gaps in the historical record.
