1857 – 1947 · THE MARCH TO SWARAJ

The Long March to Freedom

A continental awakening spanning ninety years — from mutinous barracks and tribal forests to mass non-violent resistance and naval revolts that dismantled the British Raj.

In one line: India gained independence in 1947 through ninety years of shared sacrifice, combining non-violent mass protests, armed revolutions, and tribal uprisings.

The Indian national movement was unprecedented in global history: it drew hundreds of millions of diverse people — peasants, industrial workers, forest communities, women, lawyers, and armed revolutionaries — into a shared moral battleground against foreign colonial extraction.

1857 – 1858

The Great Uprising

What this means: A massive rebellion by soldiers, farmers, and dispossessed royalty that ended East India Company control.

Triggered by soldier Mangal Pandey at Barrackpore, the revolt spread across northern and central India. Indian soldiers, peasants, and royalty like Rani Lakshmibai of Jhansi fought together. Although British forces crushed the rebellion, it forced the British Crown to take direct rule over India.

1885 – 1905

Constitutionalism & Swadeshi

What this means: The start of organized political debate and nationwide boycotts of foreign goods to promote Indian industries.

The founding of the Indian National Congress in 1885 began organized constitutional politics. When the British partitioned Bengal in 1905, Indians launched the Swadeshi movement. Citizens boycotted British textiles, opened national schools, and supported homegrown handloom weaving.

1919 – 1922

Non-Cooperation Movement

What this means: A nationwide protest where millions boycotted British courts, schools, and goods following the Amritsar massacre.

After British troops massacred peaceful protestors at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, Mahatma Gandhi launched the Non-Cooperation Movement. Millions of citizens surrendered imperial honors, left colonial courts and schools, and boycotted imported cloth.

1930 – 1931

The Salt March & Civil Disobedience

What this means: A famous 240-mile march to make tax-free salt, proving that everyday citizens could defy unjust imperial laws.

Mahatma Gandhi walked 240 miles to the sea at Dandi to make salt, breaking the British tax monopoly on basic food. This single gesture inspired millions across India to defy colonial laws peacefully, leading to the arrest of over 60,000 citizens.

1942

The Quit India Movement

What this means: A direct call demanding immediate British withdrawal from India, kept alive by underground resistance networks.

During World War II, Mahatma Gandhi issued the historic “Do or Die” call demanding an immediate British exit from India. When colonial police arrested national leaders overnight, young leaders like Aruna Asaf Ali led underground networks to continue the rebellion.

1943 – 1945

Netaji & The Azad Hind Fauj

What this means: An armed military force of Indian soldiers that marched toward India to defeat British colonial armies.

Proclaiming “Give me blood, and I will give you freedom,” Netaji Subhas Chandra Bose mobilized the Indian National Army (INA). Supported by soldiers and an all-woman regiment named after the Rani of Jhansi, the INA advanced toward northeastern frontiers at Kohima and Imphal.

1946

The Royal Indian Navy Revolt

What this means: A revolt by 20,000 Indian naval sailors that proved British forces could no longer control the military.

In February 1946, 20,000 Indian sailors on seventy-eight ships in Bombay and Karachi went on strike against racial discrimination. Supported by mill workers, the naval strike convinced British authorities that their hold on the Indian armed forces was broken.

15 August 1947

Tryst with Destiny & Partition

What this means: India achieved independence at midnight, though the triumph was accompanied by the painful displacement of Partition.

At midnight on 15 August 1947, India became an independent nation after two centuries of colonial rule. Independence arrived alongside the tragic human sorrow of Partition, which displaced fourteen million people and underscored the vital importance of protecting cultural and religious harmony.

The Many Streams of Indian Resistance

In one line: India’s freedom was won by people from all walks of life — tribal warriors, factory workers, women leaders, and non-violent protestors.

India’s independence was not achieved by one party or single region alone. It was a vast combined effort across the subcontinent. Tribal heroes like Birsa Munda in Jharkhand and Alluri Sitarama Raju in Andhra Pradesh fought to protect their ancestral lands. Factory workers held general strikes in Mumbai mills. Young revolutionaries like Bhagat Singh inspired millions with their courage. Along the northwest frontier, Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan organized Muslim non-violent volunteers (the “Red Shirts”). Across the south, leaders like C. Rajagopalachari organized mass salt marches. Every corner of India contributed to freedom.
