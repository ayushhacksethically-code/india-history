1618–1707 CE · Mughal Empire

Aurangzeb

Sixth Mughal Emperor · Expansionist · Orthodox Administrator · Contested Legacy

In one line: The last consequential Mughal emperor — who expanded the empire to its greatest extent and whose religious policies and Deccan wars remain among the most debated in Indian history.

Quick Facts

Reign 1658 – 1707 CE (49 years — longest Mughal reign)
Dynasty Mughal (Timurid)
Full Name & Title Muhi-ud-Din Muhammad Aurangzeb Alamgir (“Conqueror of the World”)
Imperial Capital Delhi (Shahjahanabad), with prolonged mobile Deccan encampment
Predecessor Shah Jahan (Father, imprisoned at Agra Fort)
Successor Bahadur Shah I (Prince Muazzam)
Key Contemporaries Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, Guru Tegh Bahadur, Dara Shikoh, Jaswant Singh, Mir Jumla, Louis XIV

Life & Reign

Muhi-ud-Din Muhammad Aurangzeb was born on 3 November 1618 at Dahod in Gujarat, the third son of Prince Khurram (later Emperor Shah Jahan) and Mumtaz Mahal. Raised in the cultured, Persianized court of Agra, Aurangzeb received rigorous instruction in Islamic theology, jurisprudence, Arabic, Persian, Turkish, and military strategy. In 1634, at age sixteen, he was awarded his first military command, famously standing his ground against a charging war elephant named Sudhakar in Agra — an act of physical courage that earned him the title Bahadur from his father. Over the next two decades, Aurangzeb served twice as Viceroy of the Deccan (1636–1644 and 1653–1658) and as governor of Gujarat and Balkh-Badakhshan in Central Asia, developing a reputation as an exceptionally disciplined general, a frugal administrator, and an austere adherent of Sunni Hanafi orthodoxy.

In September 1657, Shah Jahan fell gravely ill, triggering a brutal war of succession among his four sons: the liberal, syncretic eldest son Dara Shikoh; the governor of Bengal Shah Shuja; Aurangzeb in the Deccan; and Murad Bakhsh in Gujarat. While Dara remained at court in Delhi as the emperor's designated heir, Aurangzeb forged a tactical alliance with Murad Bakhsh, marching north with his battle-hardened Deccan veterans. Utilizing superior artillery coordination and cavalry maneuvers, Aurangzeb's forces crushed imperial armies at Dharmat (April 1658) and decisively routed Dara Shikoh at the Battle of Samugarh (May 1658). Entering Agra, Aurangzeb placed his ailing father Shah Jahan under permanent house arrest within the Agra Fort overlooking the Taj Mahal, executed Murad Bakhsh on charges of an earlier murder after neutralizing him, drove Shuja into exile in the jungles of Arakan (Burma) where he perished, and pursued Dara Shikoh to Sindh. Brought to Delhi in chains, Dara was tried by a tribunal of conservative ulema for apostasy, condemned, and executed in August 1659.

Aurangzeb formally crowned himself at Delhi in June 1659, adopting the imperial regnal title Alamgir (“Conqueror of the World”). His early reign focused on imperial consolidation and frontier stabilization. In Bengal, his capable general Mir Jumla subjugated Cooch Behar and marched up the Brahmaputra River to capture Garhgaon, the capital of the Ahom Kingdom of Assam (1662–63), though disease and fierce guerrilla resistance forced a negotiated withdrawal. In the east, Shaista Khan expelled Portuguese and Arakanese pirates from Chittagong (1666), integrating maritime Bengal firmly into the imperial economy.

A defining feature of Aurangzeb's middle reign was the gradual institutionalization of orthodox Islamic governance, marking a conscious break from the pluralistic syncretism championed by Akbar. He curtailed lavish court practices, abolishing the Persian New Year festival (Nowruz), discontinuing the Hindu practice of Jharokha Darshan (imperial balcony appearances), banning the stamping of Quranic verses on coins to prevent defilement, and dismissing court musicians and astrologers. In 1667, he commissioned the monumental Fatawa-e-Alamgiri, a thirty-volume encyclopedic codification of Hanafi Islamic jurisprudence compiled over eight years by a board of five hundred eminent Islamic scholars headed by Sheikh Nizam.

In April 1669, Aurangzeb issued general orders directing provincial governors to dismantle schools and places of worship of non-Muslims where heterodox teachings were conducted. Under this decree, prominent Hindu pilgrimage centers were demolished, including the landmark Kashi Vishwanath temple in Varanasi (August 1669) and the Keshavdeva (Krishna Janmabhoomi) temple at Mathura (January 1670), upon whose foundations imperial mosques were erected. In April 1679, Aurangzeb took the momentous step of reimposing the Jizya — the poll tax levied on non-Muslim subjects — which had been abolished by Akbar in 1564. This decision provoked public protests in Delhi and deep alienation among Rajput and Hindu merchant communities, though revenue exemptions were maintained for civil officials, women, children, and ascetics.

Aurangzeb's religious policies provoked intense political resistance across Northern and Western India. In 1669, Gokula led a massive agrarian rebellion of Jat peasants in the Mathura-Agra belt. In 1672, the armed egalitarian sect of the Satnamis rose in rebellion near Narnaul. In the Punjab, the Ninth Sikh Guru, Guru Tegh Bahadur, publicly challenged imperial religious persecution after being petitioned by Kashmiri Pandits facing forced conversion; summoned to Delhi, the Guru refused to embrace Islam and was publicly beheaded at Chandni Chowk in November 1675 by imperial order — an execution that permanently transformed the Sikh community into a militarized order (the Khalsa) under his son Guru Gobind Singh.

In Rajasthan, following the death of Maharaja Jaswant Singh of Marwar in 1678, Aurangzeb attempted to impose imperial control over Jodhpur and dispute the succession of the infant prince Ajit Singh. This precipitated a protracted twenty-five-year Rajput guerrilla war led by Durgadas Rathore and Maharana Raj Singh of Mewar. The crisis intensified in 1681 when Aurangzeb's fourth son, Prince Muhammad Akbar, rebelled against his father, issued a manifesto denouncing imperial policies, and fled south to seek alliance with the Marathas.

In late 1681, Aurangzeb moved his entire court and imperial army south into the Deccan to crush Prince Akbar, subjugate the Shia Sultanates, and destroy the rising Maratha state founded by Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj. The emperor was never to return to Northern India. Over a grueling twenty-six-year mobile campaign, Aurangzeb personally directed military operations from a massive moving tent-city (the Lashkar). He annexed the Sultanate of Bijapur after a protracted siege in 1686 and captured the impregnable fortress of Golconda through bribery in 1687, terminating the last independent Deccan sultanates and extending direct Mughal sovereignty to the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers.

In 1689, Mughal forces captured Shivaji's son and successor, Chhatrapati Sambhaji, at Sangameshwar. After refusing to renounce his faith and submit to imperial terms, Sambhaji was subjected to torturous execution at Tulapur. Rather than crushing Maratha resistance, this act galvanized the Maratha nation into an aggressive people's war of decentralized attrition under Rajaram, Tarabai, Santaji Ghorpade, and Dhanaji Jadhav. As imperial armies besieged mountain forts across the Western Ghats, Maratha roving cavalry bypassed Mughal strongpoints, intercepted supply convoys, raided deep into Malwa and Gujarat, and exacted Chauth across Mughal territories.

By the final decade of his reign, the Deccan campaign had become a catastrophic quagmire. Over two decades of relentless warfare drained the imperial treasury, disrupted agrarian production across Maharashtra, broke the lines of communication with Delhi, and exhausted imperial armies. Out of touch with northern provincial governance, the octogenarian emperor watched imperial administration unravel. Beset by illness, familial estrangement, and the looming threat of civil war among his surviving sons, Aurangzeb died in his imperial camp at Ahmadnagar on 3 March 1707 at the age of eighty-eight. In accordance with his strict personal testamentary wishes, he was buried in an unadorned open-air earthen grave at Khuldabad near Aurangabad, funded solely by the personal wages he had earned by hand-sewing prayer caps and copying the Quran.

Documented Achievements

  • Maximum Territorial Expansion: Extended the Mughal Empire to its greatest historical extent (over 3.2 million square kilometres with an estimated 150 million subjects), incorporating Bijapur, Golconda, parts of the Carnatic, and frontier Bengal into a unified imperial grid.
  • Codification of Fatawa-e-Alamgiri: Commissioned and financed the comprehensive thirty-volume encyclopedic Hanafi legal digest (Fatawa-e-Alamgiri), prepared by five hundred scholars, standardizing Islamic civil, fiscal, and criminal jurisprudence across South Asia.
  • Architectural Masterpieces: Commissioned landmark monuments of late Mughal architecture, including the grand Badshahi Mosque in Lahore (the world's largest mosque for over three centuries), the marble Moti Masjid inside the Red Fort at Delhi, and Bibi Ka Maqbara at Aurangabad.
  • Fiscal Austerity & Court Reforms: Drastically reduced imperial court expenditures, abolishing lavish Persian ceremonial displays, ending un-Islamic palace pensions, and personally funding his burial by hand-copying the Quran and sewing prayer caps.
  • Standardized Revenue Administration: Streamlined imperial revenue collection, maintained detailed audit ledgers, and curtailed corruption among provincial tax-farmers (zamindars).
  • Highest Proportion of Hindu Nobles: As demonstrated by statistical analyses by historian M. Athar Ali, the proportion of Hindu nobles (mansabdars) in the imperial administration actually rose under Aurangzeb to 31.6% (compared to 22.5% under Akbar), driven largely by the induction of Maratha and Rajput officers.
  • Long-Term Imperial Cohesion: Successfully defended and held together the world's most populous and economically productive empire against internal rebellions and European coastal expansion for nearly half a century.

Documented Atrocities & Controversies

  • Violent Fratricidal Succession: Imprisoned his father Shah Jahan in Agra Fort for eight years until death; tried and executed eldest brother Dara Shikoh for apostasy; executed younger brother Murad Bakhsh; drove brother Shah Shuja to death in exile.
  • Reimposition of Jizya (1679): Re-established the discriminatory poll tax on non-Muslim subjects more than a century after its repeal by Akbar, generating widespread economic grievance and communal alienation.
  • Systematic Temple Demolitions: Issued imperial decrees ordering the demolition of major Hindu shrines, including the Kashi Vishwanath temple at Varanasi (1669), the Keshavdeva temple at Mathura (1670), and temples in Amber, Jodhpur, and Udaipur, as recorded in court histories.
  • Judicial Execution of Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675): Ordered the public beheading of the Ninth Sikh Guru in Delhi after the Guru intervened to protect Kashmiri Pandits against forced religious conversion.
  • Torture & Execution of Sambhaji (1689): Captured and subjected Chhatrapati Sambhaji and his advisor Kavi Kalash to gruesome physical mutilation and execution at Tulapur following their refusal to convert and surrender forts.
  • Execution of Sufis & Dissenters: Ordered the execution of the unorthodox Sufi mystic Sarmad Kashani (1661) at the Jamia Masjid steps in Delhi, suppressed Shia theological practices in Golconda, and banned music performances at court.
  • Economic Exhaustion through Deccan Wars: Conducted a twenty-five-year war of attrition in the Deccan that devastated agrarian lands, drained the imperial treasury, and left the empire structurally weakened and vulnerable to rapid collapse.

Primary Evidence & Sources

Evidence — EB-Aurangzeb-1: The Reimposition of Jizya (1679)
  • Imperial Decree: The official court chronicle Maasir-i-Alamgiri records that on 2 April 1679, Aurangzeb issued an imperial farman commanding the collection of Jizya from non-Muslim subjects (zimmis) in accordance with classical Hanafi law, explicitly aimed at “curbing infidel practices and promoting Islamic order.”
  • Sympathetic/Contextual Reading: Historian Satish Chandra points out that the farman followed deep financial strain from military mobilization and was designed to rally the orthodox ulema behind the throne amidst mounting provincial unrest; poor subjects and state servants were exempted.
  • Critical Reading: Jadunath Sarkar documents widespread protests by citizens outside Delhi's Red Fort, which were crushed with war elephants, as well as protest letters from regional leaders (including Chhatrapati Shivaji) arguing that the tax violated universal justice.
Limitations: While court chroniclers framed the measure as pure religious piety, economic historians note that actual collection was uneven across outlying provinces and largely ineffective in the Deccan.
Evidence — EB-Aurangzeb-2: Documented Temple Demolitions
  • Primary Court Inscriptions: Saqi Mustaid Khan in Maasir-i-Alamgiri (pp. 81, 95–96) explicitly details the imperial orders to demolish the temple of Vishwanath at Kashi (August 1669) and the grand temple of Keshav Rai at Mathura (January 1670), noting the construction of the Gyanvapi and Shahi Eidgah mosques upon their respective sites.
  • Protective Firmans Counter-Evidence: Archives in Varanasi, Ujjain, Chitrakoot, and Guwahati contain authentic Persian firmans from Aurangzeb issuing land grants (madad-i-ma'ash) and royal protection to existing temples (e.g., Mahakaleshwar temple, Ujjain, and Balaji temple, Chitrakoot).
  • Modern Historiographical Debate: Richard Eaton's quantitative study (2000) identifies approximately eighty documented temple desecrations under Aurangzeb, arguing they were directed primarily at political rebel strongholds rather than representing blanket religious destruction; Audrey Truschke argues temples were targeted as emblems of sovereign political defiance; Jadunath Sarkar interprets them as systematic expressions of orthodox iconoclasm.
Limitations: Persian court chronicles frequently magnified temple destruction figures to burnish the emperor's orthodox credentials for Central Asian clerical audiences, while bardic regional memories often compounded local destruction narratives.
Evidence — EB-Aurangzeb-3: Execution of Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675)
  • Sikh Primary Sources: Guru Gobind Singh in the Bachittar Natak (c. 1698) writes of his father's martyrdom: “He protected the forehead mark and the sacred thread; he performed a great act in the age of darkness; he gave his head but did not give up his conviction.” Traditional Sikh historical texts (Bhat Vahis, Sri Gur Sobha) document the plea of the Kashmiri Brahmins.
  • Mughal Records: Ghulam Husain Tabatabai's Siyar-ul-Mutakherin (18th c.) records that Guru Tegh Bahadur was arrested due to armed following and political defiance in the Punjab, presenting the execution as a state security measure against potential rebellion.
  • Modern Scholarship: Historians J.S. Grewal, Khushwant Singh, and Louis Fenech analyze the trial as a collision between imperial assertions of sovereign orthodoxy and the Guru's principled defense of universal religious freedom.
Limitations: Mughal official chronicles largely suppressed direct mention of religious persecution in the capital, while later Sikh chronicles reflect post-Khalsa theological perspectives; both confirm the execution occurred in Delhi in November 1675.
Evidence — EB-Aurangzeb-4: Capture & Execution of Sambhaji (1689)
  • Mughal Court Chronicles: Maasir-i-Alamgiri and Khafi Khan's Muntakhab-ul-Lubab record that after his capture at Sangameshwar by Muqarrab Khan, Sambhaji was brought before Aurangzeb at Bahadurgarh. In response to imperial demands to surrender all forts, disclose buried treasures, and accept Islam, Sambhaji hurled defiance at the emperor and was executed by severe torture on 11 March 1689.
  • Marathi Sources: Contemporary Maratha bakhars (Jedhe Shakavali, Chitnis Bakhar) recount Sambhaji's steadfast refusal to compromise his faith and state sovereignty, immortalizing him as Dharmaveer (“Champion of Faith”).
  • Modern Historical Assessment: Stewart Gordon (1993) and Kamal Gokhale analyze the execution as a fatal tactical miscalculation: Aurangzeb believed Sambhaji's death would break the Marathas, but it instead transformed the conflict into an irrepressible guerrilla war of survival.
Limitations: Accounts of the specific verbal exchange and tortures are colored by sectarian and patriotic rhetoric, but both traditions agree on the defiance of the Maratha king and the brutality of the execution.
Evidence — EB-Aurangzeb-5: Economic & Agrarian Impact of the Deccan Wars
  • Agrarian Disruption: Irfan Habib in The Agrarian System of Mughal India demonstrates that the twenty-six-year Deccan campaign caused severe agrarian dislocation, exorbitant revenue demands on northern peasants, widespread peasant flight, and a breakdown of the jagirdari system due to a severe shortage of productive revenue assignments (be-jagiri).
  • Statistical & Treasury Depletion: Shireen Moosvi's economic studies and Angus Maddison's historical GDP estimates document that while India remained an economic giant producing ~24% of world manufacturing output under Aurangzeb, imperial cash reserves were completely consumed by military expenditures, leading to unpaid troops and mutinous garrisons.
  • Contemporary Eye-Witness Accounts: Italian traveler Niccolao Manucci and French physician Francois Bernier describe abandoned fields, famines in the Deccan following army marches, and deteriorating security on northern trade routes.
Limitations: Pre-modern economic data relies on partial fiscal ledgers and court memoirs, but all scholarly analyses confirm that the Deccan campaign catalyzed a permanent structural crisis in Mughal state finance.
Evidence — EB-Aurangzeb-6: The Historiographical Debate
  • The Orthodox Decline Model: Sir Jadunath Sarkar's foundational five-volume History of Aurangzib (1912–24) portrays Aurangzeb as a religious zealot whose religious intolerance alienated Rajputs, Sikhs, and Marathas, directly causing the downfall of the Mughal Empire.
  • The Structural & Socio-Economic Reassessment: Satish Chandra, M. Athar Ali, and Muzaffar Alam argue that the empire collapsed due to institutional, agrarian, and administrative contradictions rather than individual religious bigotry, showing that Hindu nobility numbers peaked under Aurangzeb.
  • The Contextual Revisionist Reassessment: Audrey Truschke in Aurangzeb: The Life and Legacy of India's Most Controversial King (2017) challenges colonial British communal narratives, arguing that Aurangzeb operated primarily as a pre-modern pragmatic imperialist whose actions were driven by state security and dynastic legitimacy rather than ideological Hindu-hatred.
Limitations: Modern evaluations of Aurangzeb are heavily shaped by contemporary communal politics and national identity debates; objective history requires decoupling colonial polemics from verifiable 17th-century epigraphic, Persian, and regional records.

Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj

Maratha King · Chief Adversary

Founder of the Maratha sovereign state who checked Mughal expansion in the Deccan through brilliant guerrilla strategy and mountain warfare.

Guru Tegh Bahadur

Ninth Sikh Guru · Martyr for Religious Freedom

Preached spiritual equality and was executed in Delhi in 1675 for resisting imperial religious coercion of Kashmiri Brahmins.

Dara Shikoh

Elder Brother · Syncretic Scholar

Heir-apparent of Shah Jahan who translated the Upanishads into Persian (Sirr-i-Akbar); defeated, condemned for apostasy, and executed by Aurangzeb in 1659.

Shah Jahan

Fifth Mughal Emperor · Father

Architect of the Taj Mahal and Shahjahanabad, imprisoned by Aurangzeb within Agra Fort from 1658 until his death in 1666.

Princess Jahanara Begum

Imperial Princess · Sister

Accomplished Sufi scholar and primary advisor to Shah Jahan who voluntarily shared her father's imprisonment in Agra Fort while attempting reconciliation.

Princess Roshanara Begum

Imperial Princess · Sister

Staunch political ally of Aurangzeb during the succession war who provided intelligence on Dara Shikoh's court maneuvers in Delhi.

Mirza Raja Jai Singh I

Kachhwaha King of Amber · Mughal General

Distinguished diplomat and commander who engineered the Treaty of Purandar (1665) with Shivaji before his death in Burhanpur.

King Louis XIV

Sun King of France

Contemporary European monarch whose reign (1643–1715) spanned an era of absolute monarchy, revocation of the Edict of Nantes (1685), and French diplomatic outreach to India.

Historical Legacy

Aurangzeb Alamgir remains without question the most politically and historiographically polarizing figure in the history of pre-modern South Asia. Within popular historical memory, two starkly irreconcilable caricatures dominate public discourse: in Hindu, Sikh, and Maratha cultural narratives, he is remembered as the archetypal bigot and tyrant whose iconoclastic zeal and political executions inflicted deep historical trauma; while within orthodox Islamic memory and early Pakistani nationalist historiography, he has often been venerated as a pious, ascetic monarch who defended the Islamic character of the state and halted the heterodox syncretism of his predecessors.

Modern academic scholarship has rigorously deconstructed both of these mythologies. Historians including Satish Chandra, M. Athar Ali, Richard Eaton, and Audrey Truschke demonstrate that Aurangzeb cannot be understood as an ideological cartoon. Rather, he was an astute, ruthless, and conservative imperial bureaucrat operating within the realpolitik constraints of a complex, multifaith empire. His temple destructions — while catastrophic and traumatic for local communities — were selective political sanctions targeting shrines patronized by rebellious feudal lords, coexisting paradoxically with state land grants and tax exemptions protecting dozens of non-rebellious temples. Similarly, his administration saw the highest proportion of Hindu officers in Mughal history, proving that imperial pragmatism consistently superseded religious dogmatism when state interests required it.

Nonetheless, the historical verdict on the long-term consequences of his reign is decisive. His twenty-five-year obsession with subjugating the Deccan emptied the treasury, broke the military apparatus, disrupted northern trade and governance, and alienated vital Rajput and Sikh constituencies. Within four decades of his death at Ahmadnagar, the vast centralized empire he spent forty-nine years defending fractured into autonomous regional states — the Maratha Confederacy, the Nawabs of Bengal and Awadh, the Nizam of Hyderabad, and the Sikh Misls — creating the fractured geopolitical landscape that paved the way for British colonial subjugation.
