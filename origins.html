c. 7000 – 1300 BCE · Archaeological Horizons

An Age of Many Peoples

While the Indus Valley cities rose in the northwest, the rest of the subcontinent was far from empty. From Kashmir to Karnataka, village and farming cultures were building their own worlds.

How Do We Know?

Prehistoric settlement sites were identified through surface surveys and excavation.

Dates come from radiocarbon (C-14) and thermoluminescence (TL) methods applied to organic and ceramic remains.

Both methods carry error ranges, and contaminated samples can produce anomalous dates. This is why some early dates (e.g., Bhirrana) remain disputed.

Foundational Strata

Before the Cities: The Village Roots

In one line: Long before great brick cities arose, ancient farming villages flourished across northwestern India for thousands of years.

The Indus Valley Civilization did not appear suddenly. It grew out of village life that had existed for thousands of years before. These villages practised farming, made pottery, and lived across Balochistan, the Indus plains, and the Ghaggar-Hakra valley.

At Mehrgarh in Balochistan, settled farming began around c. 7000 BCE — about 9,000 years ago, long before writing was invented. (BCE means Before Common Era, which counts backward and is the same as BC). Archaeologists found mud-brick storehouses, fields of wheat and barley, and herds of sheep and cattle. This was India’s earliest known Neolithic (New Stone Age) farming community.

Evidence
  • Archaeological: Excavations by Jarrige (1974–1986, 1997–2000), continuing under the French Archaeological Mission.
  • Archaeobotanical: Barley, wheat, and cotton remains in mud-brick granaries.
  • Faunal: Sheep, goat, and cattle remains.
  • Modern scholarship: Jarrige (1985); Meadow (1996); Possehl (2002).
Limitations: Earliest layers were disturbed by later occupation; exact date ranges depend on calibration curve choice.

At Bhirrana in Haryana, scientists tested organic remains with radiocarbon dating and tested pottery with thermoluminescence (TL) dating. Some layers dated as early as c. 7500–8000 BCE. Scholars still debate these very early dates. Nearby at Kunal (c. 6000 BCE), villagers lived in round pit-houses and made copper tools and polished beads. These settlements show the steady step-by-step move from simple hamlets toward planned towns.

Evidence
  • Archaeological: ASI excavations (Rao, 2005; Sarkar et al., 2016).
  • Dating: Radiocarbon (C-14) and thermoluminescence (TL) dates.
  • Ceramic sequence: Hakra ware ceramic sequence.
  • Modern scholarship: Sarkar et al., Nature Scientific Reports (2016).
Limitations: Charcoal from disturbed layers can produce anomalously old dates. International archaeologists remain cautious. Fuller and Agrawal have raised contamination concerns.

The urban phase is only the top layer. Below it lie millennia of village life.

How Do We Know?

Cities like Mohenjo-daro were excavated from the 1920s onward by the ASI (Marshall, 1931; Wheeler, 1968).

Their street grids, drains, and standard weights are directly observable in the archaeological record.

Their script has not been deciphered. Claims about what the Harappans believed, said, or called themselves are therefore inferences, not facts.

The Urban Exception

The Indus Valley Civilization — the urban exception

In one line: The Indus Valley Civilization was the only fully urban society in South Asia during the Bronze Age, building brick cities with planned streets and clean drainage.

The Indus Valley Civilization (IVC) flourished between c. 3300 and 1300 BCE. Its main city-building era — called the Mature Harappan period — lasted from 2600 to 1900 BCE. That was about 4,600 years ago, around the same time Egypt built the Great Pyramid at Giza. The IVC was the only Bronze Age society in the subcontinent with large planned cities.

Archaeologists have discovered over 1,000 Harappan sites. Together, they cover between 1.25 and 1.5 million square kilometres. This territory was larger than ancient Egypt or Mesopotamia at their peak.

City builders erected brick metropolises across a vast region. Sites stretched from Sutkagan Dor on the Arabian Sea coast in the west to Alamgirpur in Uttar Pradesh in the east, and from Manda in Jammu down to Daimabad in Maharashtra. Their major cities included Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, and the port city of Lothal. They built straight grid streets, multi-storey brick homes, covered drains, standard stone weights, carved seals, and the famous Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro. Their writing system remains undeciphered.

How Do We Know?

Satellite imagery (Landsat, ISRO, Sentinel) mapped the palaeochannel; geological drilling confirmed a buried river course.

This evidence shows that a river once flowed there. It does NOT show what the river was called.

The name “Saraswati” comes from the Rigveda — a text composed centuries after the Mature Harappan phase ended.

The identification is therefore an inference, not a direct observation.

Historiographical Debate

The Saraswati Debate: River, Text, and the Problem of Naming

In one line: Scientists have proven that a dried-up riverbed exists, but scholars still disagree on whether it was the ancient Vedic Saraswati.

What is being debated: Did the Indus Valley cities belong to a separate “Saraswati Civilization,” or were they all part of the same regional Indus culture?

The Rigveda speaks of the Saraswati as the greatest of rivers — mighty, nourishing, and flowing from the mountains to the sea. Modern satellites and geological digs have mapped a large dried-up riverbed — called a palaeochannel — running across Haryana, Rajasthan, and Gujarat. This ancient bed is called the Ghaggar-Hakra system. Many Harappan towns sat along its banks.

Some scholars identify this dry river with the Vedic Saraswati and speak of a distinct “Saraswati Civilization.” Other scholars argue that this link is unproven. They note that the Rigvedic Saraswati may have been a different river altogether — possibly the Helmand in Afghanistan. The question remains one of the most active debates in South Asian archaeology.

Evidence
  • Satellite imagery: Landsat-1 (Ghosh et al., 1979); ISRO studies; ALOS PALSAR; Sentinel-1A/2A.
  • Geological: Lithological drilling and isotopic fingerprinting.
  • Parliamentary record: Government of India reply (2016) stating “no scientific evidence exists to establish” a link between the palaeochannel and the Vedic Saraswati.
  • Modern scholarship (pro-identification): B.B. Lal; ASI Saraswati Heritage Project reports.
  • Modern scholarship (skeptical): Giosan et al. (2012); Sinha et al. (2013); Avikunthak (2021).
Limitations: The palaeochannel's existence is uncontested; its identity is contested. The Harappan script is undeciphered, so no contemporary text names the river.

1. Physical Evidence (Settled Science)

What the Satellite Evidence Shows

Status: Well-supported empirical science
What this means: Satellites and soil core drilling prove that an ancient river system once flowed through northwest India.
  • Landsat-1 Discovery (1979): Satellite photos (Ghosh et al., 1979) first revealed buried palaeochannels beneath the sands of the Thar Desert.
  • Multi-Sensor Geological Tracing: Later work by ISRO (Indian Space Research Organisation), Sentinel-1A, ALOS PALSAR, and Sentinel-2A satellites — combined with deep soil drilling — mapped a large river channel flowing from the Himalayan foothills to the Rann of Kutch.
  • Concentration of Settlements: Many famous Harappan cities — such as Kalibangan, Banawali, Rakhigarhi, Dholavira, and Lothal — sit directly along or near this dry channel.
  • Scientific Status: The existence of this dried-up palaeochannel network is well-supported science. It is not seriously disputed by modern geologists.
2. Epistemic Limits (Methodological)

What the Satellite Evidence Cannot Show

Status: Unproven by satellite imagery
What this means: Satellites can find old watercourses, but they cannot tell us what ancient people called them.
  • No Inherent River Name: Satellite cameras see buried mud and gravel. They cannot tell us what name ancient inhabitants used for the river.
  • Unproven Vedic Identity: Space imagery alone cannot prove that this dry riverbed was the sacred Saraswati celebrated in Vedic poetry.
  • Cultural Identity Unknown: Satellites cannot show whether the people living by the river shared the religious rituals of the Vedic hymns.
  • Parliamentary Statement (2016): In an official 2016 reply in Parliament, the Government of India stated that “no scientific evidence exists to establish” a link between this palaeochannel and the Vedic Saraswati.
  • Other Evidence Needed: Proving these claims requires written records, excavated inscriptions, or genetic data. Remote sensing alone cannot settle the question.
3. The Philological & Epigraphic Reality

The Oral-Tradition Problem

Status: The rarely-discussed, decisive linguistic angle
What this means: Ancient people memorized their hymns, and migrating tribes regularly carried old river names to new homelands.
  • Spoken Memory Over Centuries: Priests composed the Rigveda orally between c. 1500 and 1200 BCE. People did not write it down until c. 500 BCE or later. In oral cultures, names travel with moving people. Migrating tribes often name new rivers after rivers they left behind.
  • The Afghan Sister River: In the ancient Avestan language of Iran, the sister word Haraxvaiti names the Helmand river in southern Afghanistan. Leading language scholars, including Michael Witzel and Asko Parpola, argue that the early Rigvedic Saraswati may originally have been this Afghan river.
  • Undeciphered Harappan Script: The Harappan script remains undeciphered. No Harappan inscription names any river, city, or ruler. We do not know what the Harappans called themselves or their waters.
  • A Modern Retroactive Name: Writers coined the label “Saraswati Civilization” in the 20th century — thousands of years after the cities fell. It is a modern retroactive label, not an ancient fact discovered on a stone plaque.
4. The Proponent Position

The Case for a “Saraswati Civilization”

Status: Proponent archaeological & textual interpretation
What this means: Proponents argue that the high number of sites along the dry river shows a continuous indigenous culture.
  • More Sites Along the Channel: Proponents point out that hundreds of Harappan settlements sit along the Ghaggar-Hakra bed. They argue that “Indus Valley Civilization” is a misleading label.
  • Connecting Cities and Hymns: If this dry river was indeed the Rigvedic Saraswati, then the city builders and the hymn composers lived much closer in time and place than textbook dates suggest.
  • The ASI Saraswati Heritage Project: Proponents cite excavations by the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI) between 2003 and 2005 as evidence of a continuous local culture.
  • An Indigenous Continuum: They combine geology, sacred verses, and pottery styles to argue for an unbroken cultural line born inside India.
5. The Critical Position

The Case Against a Separate Civilization

Status: Mainstream archaeological & historiographical consensus
What this means: Critics argue that the cities shared one single culture, and that changing the name adds ideology rather than new facts.
  • One Unified Culture: Archaeologists use “Indus Valley Civilization” because sites across 1.5 million square kilometres shared the same pottery, city grids, and brick sizes. (“Indus” comes from Greek Indós, Persian Hinduš, and Sanskrit Sindhu. It is a neutral geographic label, whereas “Saraswati” carries modern ideological claims.)
  • A Monsoon-Fed Stream: Geological studies suggest the Ghaggar-Hakra was a seasonal, monsoon-fed river during the city period, rather than a giant snow-fed Himalayan torrent as described in hymns.
  • Parliamentary Standing Committee Critique (2004): A 2004 Parliamentary Standing Committee report criticised the ASI’s Saraswati Heritage Project as “unscientific.” The committee noted that no university body had endorsed it, and questioned building research around a “purely mythological” river.
  • Missing Archaeological Reports: Anthropologist Ashok Avikunthak (2021) in Bureaucratic Archaeology showed that key excavation reports from the project were never completed or peer-reviewed.
  • No Need for a New Name: Mainstream archaeologists view the Harappan world as one great civilization with regional flavours. They see no scientific reason to rename it.

“A river may have dried up. The argument about it has not.
The palaeochannel is real. Its name is a matter of interpretation. And three different things — the river, the text, the name — belong to three different times.”

The debate is not only about geography. It touches on questions of identity, origins, and the connection between the Harappan cities and Vedic culture. Readers are encouraged to explore both ASI excavation reports and critical scholarly analyses.

Regional Mosaic

The Other Civilizations of the Same Age

In one line: While the Indus cities thrived in the northwest, distinct farming, stone-tool, and metal-working cultures flourished across every region of India.

These were not “lesser” cultures. They were distinct civilizations suited to their own environments, and they lived at the exact same time as the Indus cities.

Kashmir

Burzahom & Gufkral

c. 3000 BCE onward (about 5,000 years ago)

People dug round underground pit-houses to stay warm during cold Himalayan winters. They made polished bone needles, farmed early crops, and buried domestic dogs with care.

Ganga Plain

Lahuradewa & Chirand

c. 7000–2000 BCE (about 9,000 to 4,000 years ago)

Farmers here grew some of the earliest domesticated rice in world history. They built settled villages with clay huts and polished bone tools.

Uttar Pradesh

Koldihwa & Mahagara

c. 2500–1500 BCE (Belan Valley)

Villagers made handmade pottery decorated with cord impressions, built circular huts of wood and clay, and cultivated early rice strains.

The 7th-millennium BCE dates proposed by G.R. Sharma (1980) are no longer accepted by mainstream scholarship after radiocarbon recalibration (Possehl 1999; Fuller 2006).

Evidence
  • Archaeobotanical: Phytolith and macroremain studies (Tewari et al., 2006; Fuller, 2006).
  • Radiocarbon recalibration: Possehl (1999); Fuller (2006) place Koldihwa's settled Neolithic at c. 2500–1500 BCE.
  • Modern scholarship: Fuller and colleagues' work on South Asian rice domestication.
Limitations: Distinguishing wild gathering (Oryza nivara) from cultivated (Oryza sativa) rice in early layers is difficult.
Rajasthan

Ahar–Banas Culture

c. 3000–1500 BCE (Copper Age)

A skilled metal-working community that built copper smelting furnaces, cast flat axes, and made distinctive black-and-red pottery painted with white patterns.

Madhya Pradesh

Kayatha & Malwa

c. 2400–1700 BCE

Farming towns fortified with stockades, storing copper bangles, semi-precious stone beads, and finely chipped tiny stone blades (microliths).

Maharashtra

Jorwe Culture

c. 1400–1000 BCE (about 3,400 years ago)

Late Copper-Age farming villages famous for pottery jars with elegant pouring spouts, multi-room mud houses, and communal grain storehouses.

Karnataka

Brahmagiri, Hallur, Kupgal

c. 3000–1000 BCE

Pastoral cattle herders built large ash-mounds from animal pens, carved rock art on granite boulders, and later raised large stone monuments (Megaliths).

Deccan

Daimabad & Inamgaon

c. 2300–1000 BCE

Prosperous farming and craft towns that dug irrigation channels, ran metal workshops, and cast famous heavy bronze statues of chariots and rhinos.

What Scholars Still Debate

In one line: Archaeologists still debate early village dates, foreign trade connections, and how rural communities grew into city builders.

Bhirrana Dates: Scientific tests at Bhirrana produced dates as old as c. 7500–8000 BCE. Some archaeologists accept these early dates, while others urge caution because old soil samples can easily be contaminated. All scholars agree that the mature cities emerged around 2600 BCE.

Local Growth vs. Foreign Trade: Scholars still debate how much Indus city building grew out of local Indian village traditions, and how much it was inspired by sea trade with Mesopotamia and the Persian Gulf.

From Village Beds to Great Cities: Archaeological layers show that people lived continuously along the dry Ghaggar-Hakra riverbed. Their culture evolved step by step rather than being wiped out or replaced by invaders.

The story is still being discovered. New excavations continue to refine our timeline.

How Do We Know?

The Rigveda was transmitted orally for centuries before being written down. Its composition date is inferred from philology, not archaeology.

Indo-Aryan language speakers entered South Asia around 2000–1500 BCE, per archaeogenetic evidence (Narasimhan et al., 2019).

These two lines of evidence — textual and genetic — converge on a shared window, but neither alone is decisive.

Civilizational Continuity

From Village to Veda

In one line: When the big cities declined around 1900 BCE, local farming adaptations overlapped with Central Asian pastoral migrations, giving rise to the Vedic era.

Around 1900 to 1300 BCE, rainfall patterns changed and rivers shifted course. People gradually left the big northwestern cities and moved east toward the Punjab and the fertile Ganga plains. Life did not stop. Instead, families adapted by settling into smaller farming villages.

At the same time, linguistic and archaeogenetic evidence indicates that speakers of Indo-Aryan languages migrated into the subcontinent from the Central Asian steppe between approximately 2000 and 1500 BCE. Their arrival overlapped with the Late Harappan phase and with the composition of the earliest Vedic hymns. Genetic studies (Narasimhan et al., Science, 2019) show that modern South Asian populations are a mix of ancestral Iranian-farmer-related, Andamanese-related, and Steppe-related ancestries.

The scale, timing, and social dynamics of this migration remain debated. The site presents it as mainstream scholarly consensus, not as settled fact.

Evidence
  • Archaeogenetic: Narasimhan et al., Science (2019); Shinde et al., Cell (2019).
  • Linguistic: Comparative Indo-European philology (Witzel, 1995; Parpola, 2015).
  • Textual: Rigvedic references to geography, horses, and chariots.
Limitations: The scale, timing, and social dynamics of the migration remain debated. Some scholars emphasize gradual integration; others argue for a more complex picture involving competition and cultural negotiation.

During the Vedic Period (c. 1500–500 BCE — about 3,500 to 2,500 years ago), pastoral communities composed the sacred hymns of the Rigveda in early Sanskrit. Over centuries, thinkers composed the Brahmanas and the philosophical dialogues of the Upanishads. These communities lived alongside iron-working kingdoms, southern stone-monument builders (Megaliths), and eastern rice farmers in an ever-diverse land.
