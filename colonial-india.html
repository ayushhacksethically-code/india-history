1757–1947 · COMPANY RULE TO CROWN RULE

Colonial India

How the British came, how they ruled, and what they changed

In one line: The British ruled India for nearly two centuries — first through a trading company, then through the Crown. Their rule reshaped India’s economy, society, and politics in ways that still matter today.

Commercial Venture to Empire

How the British Arrived

In one line: A private London corporation granted a royal monopoly in 1600 gradually transformed from seafaring merchants into the sovereign military rulers of the Indian subcontinent.

The British presence in the Indian subcontinent did not originate as a state project of the British Crown, but as a joint-stock commercial venture. On 31 December 1600, Queen Elizabeth I granted a royal charter to the Governor and Company of Merchants of London Trading into the East Indies — known to history as the East India Company (EIC). For over a century, the Company operated strictly within the diplomatic and commercial framework of the Mughal Empire. Company merchants established fortified trading posts (“factories”) along the Indian coastline: Surat in Gujarat (1612), Fort St. George in Madras (1644), Bombay leased from King Charles II (1668), and Fort William in Calcutta (1690). These outposts existed to purchase Indian textiles, raw silk, indigo, saltpetre, and spices in exchange for European silver bullion.

The fragmentation of centralized Mughal authority following the death of Emperor Aurangzeb in 1707 altered this mercantile relationship. As regional successor states — the Nawabs of Bengal, the Nizams of Hyderabad, the Maratha Confederacy, and the Kingdom of Mysore — competed for subcontinental hegemony, European trading companies exploited internal rivalries. The decisive transition from commerce to sovereign power took place in fertile Bengal. In June 1757, at the Battle of Plassey (Palashi), Lieutenant-Colonel Robert Clive conspired with Mir Jafar, the disgruntled commander of Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah’s army, alongside the wealthy Jagat Seth banking house. Clive defeated Siraj-ud-Daulah and installed Mir Jafar as a puppet ruler, securing immense personal fortunes and territorial revenues for the Company.

The definitive legal foundation of British imperial dominion was cemented seven years later at the Battle of Buxar (October 1764). Major Hector Munro defeated the combined forces of Nawab Mir Qasim of Bengal, Nawab Shuja-ud-Daulah of Awadh, and Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II. The resulting Treaty of Allahabad (1765) granted the Company the formal imperial office of Diwani — the absolute right to assess and collect all land revenue across Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa, encompassing over twenty million agrarian producers. The commercial corporation had officially become a fiscal sovereign.

Over the following nine decades, the Company expanded across the subcontinent through aggressive diplomacy, military modernization, and administrative contrivance. Under Governor-General Lord Wellesley (1798–1805), the Subsidiary Alliance compelled Indian rulers to disband their own armed forces, subsidize British troop garrisons within their borders, and forfeit external foreign relations. Through four brutal Anglo-Mysore Wars (culminating in the death of Tipu Sultan at Srirangapatna in 1799), three Anglo-Maratha Wars (ending in the annexation of the Peshwa’s territories in 1818), and two Anglo-Sikh Wars (leading to the annexation of the Punjab in 1849), the Company dismantled every peer military rival. Under Lord Dalhousie (1848–1856), the Doctrine of Lapse legally disinherited adopted heirs, annexing historic kingdoms such as Satara, Sambalpur, Nagpur, and Jhansi, before crowning the conquest with the annexation of Awadh on grounds of “misgovernance” in 1856. By the eve of 1857, nearly the entire subcontinent was subordinate to Company rule.

“The Court of Directors had given them no instruction to conquer an empire. It was an accident of trade, converted by military adventurers and commercial accountants into the most lucrative dominion the world had ever seen.”
Edmund Burke, Parliamentary Speeches on the Impeachment of Warren Hastings (1788)
Imperial Rupture

The 1857 Turning Point

In one line: A violent, widespread revolt by Indian soldiers and dispossessed rulers shattered the East India Company, prompting the British Parliament to abolish company administration and bring India under the direct rule of Queen Victoria.

In May 1857, long-simmering discontent erupted into open revolt. Indian sepoys of the Bengal Army stationed at Meerut mutinied, killed British officers, and marched directly to Delhi, where they proclaimed the aging Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar as the legitimate sovereign of Hindustan. While the immediate trigger was the introduction of the Enfield rifle cartridge — rumoured to be greased with beef tallow and pig lard, violating both Hindu and Muslim religious taboos — the uprising was propelled by deep structural grievances: the destruction of regional princely states, punitive agrarian land revenue demands, evangelical missionary arrogance, and racial discrimination within the military ranks.

The rebellion rapidly spread across northern and central India, transforming from a military mutiny into a civil insurrection. Key leaders emerged across regional centres: Rani Lakshmibai in Jhansi, Nana Saheb and Tantia Tope in Kanpur, Begum Hazrat Mahal in Awadh, and Kunwar Singh in Bihar. The character of 1857 remains contested among modern historians: British colonial historians characterized it as a self-interested “Sepoy Mutiny,” early nationalist historians such as V.D. Savarkar hailed it as the “First War of Indian Independence,” while Marxist and subaltern scholars analyze it as a complex confluence of peasant rebellion, religious anxiety, and feudal restorationism.

The British crushed the rebellion through savage reprisal, mass executions, and the sack of Delhi. The political consequences were immediate and total. Recognizing that a private commercial corporation could no longer govern an empire, the British Parliament enacted the Government of India Act 1858. The East India Company was stripped of all administrative authority and liquidated. Direct sovereign control was transferred to the British Crown. Queen Victoria issued her Royal Proclamation on 1 November 1858, promising non-interference in Indian religious practices and equal protection under imperial law — promises that were frequently contradicted by everyday administrative practice. The Governor-General was given the title of Viceroy, marking the birth of the formal British Raj.

Administrative Statecraft

How the British Ruled

In one line: The British Raj operated through an autocratic bureaucratic “steel frame,” a unified legal code, an army staffed by Indians but officered by Britons, and political strategies that institutionalized caste and communal divisions.

The imperial apparatus of the British Raj rested upon an institutional hierarchy designed for centralized authority and swift revenue collection. At the apex sat the Secretary of State for India in London — a British Cabinet minister answerable only to the British Parliament. In Calcutta (and after 1911, New Delhi), the Viceroy and Governor-General ruled alongside an Executive Council. The administrative backbone was the elite Indian Civil Service (ICS), famously termed the “steel frame” of the empire. Composed of roughly one thousand officers presiding over three hundred million people, the ICS was recruited through competitive examinations held exclusively in London until 1922, ensuring that higher administrative echelons remained almost entirely British until the twilight of colonial rule.

Colonial authority was consolidated through legal codification. Under the leadership of Thomas Babington Macaulay, the First Law Commission drafted the Indian Penal Code (1860), followed by the Code of Criminal Procedure (1861) and the Indian Evidence Act (1872). These statutes replaced the plural traditions of Islamic sharia, Hindu dharmashastra, and local customary dispute resolution with a uniform, state-administered statutory code. While the British claimed this established the impartial “Rule of Law,” colonial jurisprudence consistently protected European officials from prosecution by Indian judges — a racial privilege that sparked fierce European backlash during the 1883 Ilbert Bill controversy.

The ultimate guarantor of imperial dominance was the Indian Army. Following 1857, the military was restructured on the basis of the “Martial Races” theory. Colonial ethnographers classified specific ethnic groups — particularly Gurkhas, Sikhs, Pathans, and Punjabi Musalmans — as naturally warlike, while disqualifying Bengalis and other politically active groups as “effeminate.” Units were organized into racially segregated regiments commanded exclusively by British commissioned officers. Artillery was kept strictly out of Indian hands, ensuring that internal mutiny would be technologically impossible.

In cultural policy, the British abandoned traditional patronage in favor of English-medium instruction. In his celebrated 1835 Minute on Indian Education, Macaulay argued that European literature and science were unconditionally superior to Asian learning, advocating the creation of an anglicized intermediary class. Infrastructure followed strategic imperatives: the Indian Railways (inaugurated between Bombay and Thane in April 1853), the electric telegraph network, and the uniform penny postal system were financed through guaranteed returns to British investors, designed fundamentally to rush troops to rebellious frontiers and transport raw cotton, wheat, and coal from the hinterland to coastal shipping ports.

Finally, imperial governance relied upon the policy of divide and rule (divide et impera). The colonial state codified social boundaries through decennial censuses, classifying Indians strictly by religion and caste. In the Morley-Minto Reforms of 1909, the British instituted separate electorates for Muslims, creating institutional incentives for politicians to organize along communal rather than shared secular lines — a structural wedge that poisoned subcontinental politics for the next four decades.

“We must at present do our best to form a class who may be interpreters between us and the millions whom we govern; a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect.”
Thomas Babington Macaulay, Minute on Indian Education (1835)
Extractive Economy

Economic Impact: Drain, Deindustrialization, and Famines

In one line: British economic policies dismantled India’s global manufacturing supremacy, drained billions of pounds into metropolitan Britain, and subjected rural millions to catastrophic, policy-driven famines.

In the early eighteenth century, before British conquest, the Indian subcontinent produced an estimated 25 to 27 percent of global industrial manufacturing output, driven by world-renowned handloom textiles, shipbuilding, and metallurgy. By 1947, India’s share of world industrial production had plummeted to less than 2 percent. This collapse was not the inevitable result of natural market forces, but the direct outcome of imperial fiscal and commercial policy — a historical process termed deindustrialization. Britain imposed heavy import tariffs (up to 70–80%) on Indian textiles entering the British market, while compelling India to accept cheap, machine-spun yarn and cloth from Lancashire and Manchester duty-free. Millions of traditional handloom weavers in Bengal, Dacca, and Surat were ruined and driven onto overcrowded agricultural land to survive.

The structural transfer of subcontinental surplus to Britain was first comprehensively demonstrated by nationalist pioneer Dadabhai Naoroji in his landmark treatise Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901). Naoroji articulated the “Drain of Wealth” theory: India consistently exported far more goods than it imported, yet the trade surplus never returned as capital. Instead, it was remitted to London under the euphemism of “Home Charges” — paying for the Secretary of State’s office, pensions for British military officers and civil servants, interest on guaranteed railway debts, and the imperial military campaigns fought in Burma, China, and Afghanistan. Modern economic historians, notably Professor Utsa Patnaik, calculate that the net drain from India to Great Britain between 1765 and 1938 amounted to approximately £1.8 billion in historic terms (equivalent to nearly $45 trillion in modern purchasing value).

To maximize extraction, the British instituted rigid, cash-based land revenue settlements that monetized rural life:

  • The Permanent Settlement (1793): Instituted by Lord Cornwallis across Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa, fixing land revenue in perpetuity and elevating hereditary revenue collectors into absolute landlords (zamindars), dispossessing the traditional peasantry of their customary rights.
  • The Ryotwari System: Devised by Thomas Munro in Madras and Bombay, assessing land tax directly on individual peasant cultivators (ryots) at punishing rates that frequently absorbed 45 to 55 percent of gross yield.
  • The Mahalwari System: Deployed across the North-Western Provinces and the Punjab, holding entire village communities jointly responsible for revenue quotas.

These rigid fiscal demands forced peasant farmers to substitute food grains (millet, rice) with commercial cash crops (indigo, opium, jute, and raw cotton). When monsoon droughts struck, the population possessed zero food reserves and zero purchasing power. Under rigid Victorian dogmas of laissez-faire and market non-intervention, the colonial state refused to halt food exports during severe droughts. Catastrophic, policy-amplified famines repeatedly struck the subcontinent: the Great Bengal Famine of 1770 (which killed an estimated 10 million people, one-third of the province), the Great Famine of 1876–1878 (under Viceroy Lord Lytton, which claimed 6 to 10 million lives while India exported record shipments of wheat to England), the devastating famines of 1896–1897 and 1899–1900, and the wartime Bengal Famine of 1943 (where 2.1 to 3 million civilians perished as wartime diversion and scorched-earth policies withheld food supplies under the Churchill war cabinet).

“The drain consists of two elements: first, that arising from the remittances by European officials of their savings, and for their expenditure in England; second, that arising from remittances by non-official Europeans. What is the consequence? The country is being bled to death.”
Dadabhai Naoroji, Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901)
Living Connection

The railways the British built still run today. But so does the memory of the famines that killed millions. Both are colonial legacies.

Social Transformation

Social Impact: Caste, Reform, and the Modern Sphere

In one line: Colonial rule sparked contentious social reform movements, fostered an English-educated intelligentsia, and transformed fluid community practices into rigid administrative categories.

The encounter with colonial power deeply disrupted Indian social hierarchy. Prior to the late nineteenth century, caste identities (jatis) were locally negotiated, occupational, and relatively fluid across geography. Beginning with the first all-India decennial census in 1871, led by British administrator-ethnographers like Sir Herbert Risley, the colonial state attempted to classify, tabulate, and rank every caste according to rigid orthodox Brahminical hierarchy (varna). Caste was codified into court proceedings, land tenure laws, and government employment rosters. Rather than observing an existing static system, colonial categorization hardened and ossified what had previously been flexible social boundaries.

At the same time, colonial contact generated intense self-examination and reform movements among Indian intellectuals. Early nineteenth-century reformers interrogated indigenous practices using both ancient Sanskrit sources and modern Enlightenment rationality. In Bengal, Raja Ram Mohan Roy founded the Brahmo Samaj in 1828, campaigning tirelessly against the practice of sati (the burning of widows, which was concentrated primarily in parts of Bengal and Rajasthan), leading to the Sati Regulation Act of 1829 under Governor-General Lord William Bentinck. Decades later, Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar marshaled traditional Vedic scriptures to advocate for the Hindu Widows’ Remarriage Act of 1856, establishing schools for girls and combating child marriage.

In western India, social reform challenged the very roots of caste hierarchy. In 1848, Jyotirao Phule and his wife Savitribai Phule opened the subcontinent’s first indigenously run school for girls from marginalized castes in Pune, going on to establish the Satyashodhak Samaj (1873) to dismantle Brahminical hegemony. In Maharashtra and later across the country, pioneer scholars like Pandita Ramabai established the Sharada Sadan (1889), providing secular education, vocational independence, and shelter for high-caste child widows.

Christian missionary activity, legalized by the Charter Act of 1813, triggered defensive socio-religious revivalism across communities. Reformers established the Arya Samaj (Dayananda Saraswati, 1875) promoting Vedic purity, the Ramakrishna Mission (Swami Vivekananda, 1897) articulating universal Vedantic humanism, the Deoband Movement (1866) focusing on orthodox Islamic legal renewal, and the Aligarh Movement (Sir Syed Ahmad Khan, 1875) advocating modern scientific education for Muslims through the Anglo-Oriental College.

The expansion of English education inadvertently forged an intellectual class of Indian lawyers, journalists, teachers, and civil servants. Steeped in European philosophy — John Locke, John Stuart Mill, and the ideals of the American and French Revolutions — this new middle class turned British political concepts against British colonial practice. Demanding equality before the law, representation, and civil liberties, they established the institutional platforms that would direct the anti-colonial struggle.

Living Connection

The caste categories used in modern affirmative action are partly inherited from the 1871 colonial census — a British administrative tool that hardened what had been fluid.

Historical Assessment

Legacy of Colonial Rule

In one line: Colonial rule bequeathed modern institutional infrastructure alongside profound economic impoverishment, hardened communal identities, and the trauma of territorial division.

The historical assessment of British colonial rule remains deeply contested. Imperial apologists (such as Niall Ferguson) often assert that the British Raj was a benevolent, modernizing force that brought political unification, parliamentary institutions, Western science, a unified criminal code, and 65,000 kilometres of railway track to an otherwise fragmented land.

In contrast, nationalist, Marxist, and post-colonial historians (from Dadabhai Naoroji and R.C. Dutt to Bipan Chandra, Irfan Habib, and Shashi Tharoor) demonstrate that modern developments were incidental byproducts, not the purpose, of the colonial state. The railways were constructed at extortionate guaranteed interest rates charged to Indian taxpayers, designed primarily to transport imperial troops to border conflicts and raw commodities to export docks. The legal system safeguarded British property rights and protected European racial privilege.

What the British took is quantifiable in economic ruin and human loss: the systemic drainage of national wealth, the collapse of indigenous handicrafts, massive agrarian debt, and tens of millions of preventable famine deaths. What they hardened was equally destructive: fixed caste classifications that reinforced graded inequality, and separate political electorates that fostered competitive sectarian antagonism, ultimately culminating in the blood-drenched catastrophe of Partition in 1947.

Independent India did not reject the instruments of the colonial state; rather, it democraticized them. The Indian Civil Service became the Indian Administrative Service, the colonial army became a professional republican military subordinate to civil democracy, and the English language became an indispensable domestic and international bridge. Understanding colonial rule is essential not to harbour resentment, but to comprehend the institutional machinery, social struggles, and constitutional protections of the modern Indian Republic.
